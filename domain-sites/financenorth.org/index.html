Our approach

Finance North focuses on durable principles and official sources rather than hot tips. We don't sell financial products, and we don't publish unverified numbers, such as a predicted PFD amount. When something depends on current rules, we tell you where to check: the PFD Division, the IRS, your local assessor or your utility.

Our three core habits for northern finances: budget annually (because Alaska costs are seasonal), use sinking funds for heat, tires and travel, and treat the PFD as a bonus rather than regular income.

Frequently asked questions

Does Alaska have a state income tax?

No. Alaska repealed its personal income tax in 1980. You still owe federal income tax, and the PFD counts as federal taxable income.

When can I apply for the PFD?

The application period is usually January 1 through March 31 each year, through the PFD Division at pfd.alaska.gov. You generally must have been a resident for the entire previous calendar year.

Why is the cost of living so high in Alaska?

Most goods travel long distances by ship, barge, truck or plane, energy costs are high in many areas, and small markets have less competition. Costs are highest in rural communities off the road system.

What's the cheapest way to heat a home in Alaska?

It depends on local fuel prices and your equipment. Natural gas is often cheapest where available; heat pumps can be economical where electricity is moderately priced. Compare using cost per million BTU.

Should I count the PFD in my monthly budget?

A cautious approach is not to. The amount changes yearly, so many households treat it as a once-a-year bonus for taxes, emergency savings, debt payoff or winter heating.

No. Our guides are general education only. For advice about your specific situation, consult a licensed financial professional or tax preparer.
