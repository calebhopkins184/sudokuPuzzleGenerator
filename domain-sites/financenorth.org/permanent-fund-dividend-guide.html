Alaska Permanent Fund Dividend (PFD): A Plain-Language Guide

Updated November 2, 2026 · By the Finance North team

Disclaimer: This guide is for general educational purposes only and is not financial, tax or legal advice. Finance North is not a financial institution or licensed advisory firm. Rules and amounts change; confirm details with official sources and consult a licensed professional about your situation.

The Permanent Fund Dividend (PFD) is an annual payment to eligible Alaska residents, funded by earnings from the Alaska Permanent Fund, a state savings fund built largely from oil revenue. To qualify, you generally must have been an Alaska resident for the entire previous calendar year and intend to remain indefinitely. Applications are usually accepted from January 1 through March 31, and the amount is set each year through the state budget process. The PFD counts as taxable income on your federal return.

Where the PFD comes from

Alaska voters approved a constitutional amendment in 1976 creating the Permanent Fund, which receives a share of the state's mineral royalties. The fund is invested by the Alaska Permanent Fund Corporation, a state-owned corporation. The first dividends were paid in 1982. The dividend program itself is run by the PFD Division of the Alaska Department of Revenue.

Historically, the dividend amount came from a statutory formula based on the fund's earnings. In recent years, the Alaska Legislature has set the amount during the annual budget process, and it has varied considerably from year to year. Some years have also included one-time energy relief payments on top of the dividend. Because the amount is a political and budget decision, it's wise not to count on a particular figure until it's announced.

Who is eligible?

The PFD Division publishes the official eligibility rules, which are detailed. In broad terms, to receive a dividend you must:

  • Have been an Alaska resident for the entire "qualifying year" (the calendar year before you apply)
  • Intend to remain an Alaska resident indefinitely when you apply
  • Not have claimed residency in another state or country, or obtained benefits based on residency elsewhere, during the qualifying year
  • Not have been absent from Alaska beyond what the rules allow (there are specific allowable absences, such as for school, military service or medical treatment, with conditions)
  • Not have been sentenced or incarcerated for certain criminal offenses during the qualifying year
  • Be physically present in Alaska, or on an allowable absence, at certain times

If you moved to Alaska partway through a year, that year doesn't count as a full qualifying year. For example, someone who arrives in mid-year usually becomes eligible to apply in the second January after moving. Children can qualify too; a parent or guardian applies on their behalf.

How and when to apply

  1. Apply during the application period, usually January 1 to March 31, through the PFD Division's website (pfd.alaska.gov) or on paper.
  2. Choose direct deposit for faster payment, and double-check your bank routing and account numbers.
  3. Check your application status online. Statuses like "eligible-not-paid" indicate an approved application waiting for the next payment date.
  4. Respond quickly if the Division asks for more documentation. Missed deadlines are a common reason for denials.
  5. Appeal if needed. If you're denied and believe it's an error, the PFD Division explains the appeal process and deadlines in its denial notice.

Payments to approved applicants are typically issued in the fall, with additional payment dates for applications approved later. The Division announces dates each year.

Is the PFD taxable?

Yes. The PFD is taxable income for federal income tax purposes and must be reported on your federal return. Alaska itself has no state personal income tax. A few points that trip people up:

  • Children's dividends are the child's income. Depending on the child's total unearned income, the child may need to file a return, and the IRS "kiddie tax" rules may apply. See IRS guidance on unearned income of children.
  • Withholding: Taxes generally aren't withheld from the PFD automatically, so a large dividend can mean a larger tax bill or smaller refund. Some people set aside a portion for taxes.
  • Energy relief payments paid with the dividend in some years have also been treated as taxable income.
  • Benefits: Receiving a PFD can affect eligibility for some means-tested benefits. Alaska has a program to help some public assistance recipients avoid losing benefits; ask the agency that administers your benefits.

Garnishment and deductions

The PFD can be garnished, or "levied," for certain debts, such as unpaid child support, court-ordered restitution and some judgments and state debts. If your dividend is subject to a levy, the PFD Division notifies you. If you expect a garnishment, plan around a smaller payment.

Alaskans can also choose to donate part of their dividend to eligible Alaska nonprofits through the state's Pick.Click.Give. program when they apply.

Smart ways to use your PFD

Because the PFD arrives as a lump sum once a year, it's an opportunity to make a meaningful financial move rather than letting it disappear into everyday spending. Common priorities, roughly in order:

  1. Set aside money for federal taxes on the dividend if you won't have enough withheld from other income.
  2. Build or top up an emergency fund. In Alaska, surprise expenses often include vehicle repairs, a dead furnace or last-minute flights.
  3. Prepay winter heating. Some households buy a load of heating oil or propane, or a season of firewood, in the fall when the PFD arrives.
  4. Pay down high-interest debt, such as credit cards.
  5. Save for kids' futures. Many families put children's dividends into savings, a 529 education plan or a custodial account. Alaska's 529 programs are worth comparing with other states' plans.
  6. Retirement savings, such as contributing to an IRA. Check IRS rules for current contribution limits and eligibility.
  7. Planned big purchases, such as travel Outside, a needed vehicle repair or home weatherization, paid in cash instead of on credit.

Watch for PFD scams. Fraudsters sometimes impersonate the PFD Division by phone, text or email, asking for your PIN, bank information or a "fee" to release your dividend. The official site is pfd.alaska.gov. When in doubt, contact the Division directly using contact information from that official site.

Frequently asked questions

How much is this year's PFD?

The amount is set each year through the state budget process and announced by the State of Alaska. Check the PFD Division at pfd.alaska.gov for the official current figure rather than relying on news estimates.

Do I have to pay taxes on my PFD?

Yes, it's taxable on your federal income tax return. Alaska has no state income tax, so there's no state tax on it.
