Garage floor guide

Epoxy vs. Polyaspartic Garage Floor Coatings for Cold Alaska Garages

Last updated November 2, 2026

For an Alaska garage, the best floor coating is the one applied to a properly ground, dry, warm-enough slab. Epoxy and polyaspartic systems can both work well; the main differences are how they handle cold temperatures, UV, and how fast they cure. Most garage coating failures trace back to preparation and temperature, not the brand.

Epoxy vs. polyaspartic: which is better for a cold-climate garage?

Polyaspartic coatings generally tolerate lower application temperatures, cure in hours instead of days and resist yellowing from sunlight. Epoxy is usually less expensive, gives a longer working time and builds thickness well, but most epoxies need a warmer slab and cure slowly in the cold. Many professional systems combine the two: an epoxy base coat with a polyaspartic or polyurethane topcoat.

These are general tendencies. Formulations vary widely, so the product's technical data sheet is the final word on temperature limits and recoat windows.

Why Alaska garages are hard on coatings

  • Cold slabs. Unheated garage slabs stay cold well into spring. Coatings applied below their minimum temperature may never cure properly.
  • Slush and de-icers. Vehicles drag in snow, gravel and road chemicals. Meltwater sits on the floor for months, testing adhesion at any weak spot.
  • Studs and gravel. Studded tires and grit abrade the surface. A tough topcoat and broadcast aggregate help.
  • Hot tire pickup. Warm tires can soften and lift thin or poorly bonded coatings. Good prep and an appropriate system largely prevent it.
  • Slipperiness. A glossy coating plus meltwater is slick. Plan for anti-slip aggregate, especially near doors.

Surface preparation: the step that decides success

Concrete should be mechanically profiled, usually by diamond grinding or shot blasting, to open the pores and remove weak surface material. Acid etching kits are common for DIY but give a less consistent profile and leave residue if not rinsed well.

  1. Test for moisture. Tape a plastic sheet to the slab for at least a day (the simple ASTM D4263 method). Condensation under it or darkened concrete means moisture is coming up. Professionals may use calcium chloride (ASTM F1869) or in-slab relative humidity tests (ASTM F2170). New concrete commonly needs at least about 28 days to cure before coating, often longer.
  2. Clean and degrease. Remove oil, tire marks and any sealer. Many coatings won't bond over existing sealers.
  3. Profile. Grind or shot blast to a light-to-medium profile, often described with the ICRI concrete surface profile scale (CSP), and vacuum thoroughly.
  4. Repair. Fill cracks, spalls and pits with a compatible repair material. Honor control joints rather than bridging moving cracks with a rigid coat.

Temperature and humidity: getting the timing right

  • Warm the slab, not just the air. In a heatable garage, bring the space up to temperature a day or more ahead so the concrete itself warms. Use an infrared thermometer to check the slab.
  • Stay above the dew point. A common industry rule is that the surface should be at least 5°F above the dew point to avoid condensation forming under or on the coating.
  • Avoid unvented combustion heaters. Unvented propane or kerosene heaters add moisture and carbon monoxide, and open flames are a fire hazard around coating vapors. Ventilate as the product label requires.
  • Plan for summer if unheated. In an unheated garage, the warm stretch from roughly June through August gives the widest window for epoxy.

A typical professional system

  1. Moisture testing and mechanical grinding.
  2. Crack and spall repair.
  3. Base coat (epoxy or polyaspartic), often with decorative vinyl flakes broadcast to refusal.
  4. Scraping and vacuuming of excess flake.
  5. One or more clear polyaspartic or polyurethane topcoats, with anti-slip additive where needed.

Caring for a coated garage floor in winter

  • Squeegee meltwater toward the drain or door rather than letting it evaporate and leave chemical residue.
  • Rinse de-icer residue periodically with water and a pH-neutral cleaner.
  • Use a containment mat under vehicles if your floor has no drain.
  • Put pads under jack stands, kickstands and trailer tongues.

Frequently asked questions

Can you epoxy a garage floor in the winter in Alaska?

Only if the slab itself can be kept warm enough, typically by heating the garage well before, during and after application. In an unheated garage, wait for summer or use a product rated for colder slabs.

Why did my garage floor coating peel?

The most common causes are inadequate surface preparation, moisture coming up through the slab, coating over an old sealer, or applying below the product's minimum temperature.

Is polyaspartic worth the extra cost?

It can be if you need a fast return to service, plan to apply in cooler conditions, or want a color-stable finish. A properly prepared epoxy system can also perform well in a garage with stable temperatures.
