Spray Foam Insulation Basics for Alaska Homes

Last updated November 2, 2026

Spray polyurethane foam (SPF) insulates and air-seals in one step, which is valuable in Alaska's long heating season. Choosing between open-cell and closed-cell foam, and handling vapor control correctly for a cold climate, matters more than the brand. Installation is chemical work best left to trained, equipped installers.

Open-cell vs. closed-cell spray foam

Closed-cell foam is denser, delivers more R-value per inch (roughly R-6 to R-7), and at typical installed thicknesses acts as a vapor retarder. Open-cell foam is lighter, cheaper per inch, vapor-permeable and lower in R-value (roughly R-3.5 to R-4 per inch). In Alaska's cold climate, closed-cell is the more common choice where vapor control is needed.

Vapor control in Alaska's climate zones

Most of Alaska falls in climate zones 7 and 8 under the International Energy Conservation Code; Anchorage is in zone 7 and much of the Interior and North is zone 8. In these zones, warm, moist indoor air pushes outward through walls and ceilings for most of the year. If it reaches a cold surface, it condenses, which can lead to mold and rot.

  • Closed-cell foam against the sheathing keeps the sheathing warmer and blocks vapor at the same time.
  • Flash-and-batt (a layer of closed-cell foam topped with fiberglass or mineral wool) can work, but the foam layer must be thick enough to keep its inside face above the dew point. Cold-climate guidance calls for a larger share of foam in colder zones.
  • Open-cell foam in exterior walls or roofs generally requires a separate vapor retarder on the warm side in these zones.

Local codes and programs set specific requirements. In Alaska, homes financed through the Alaska Housing Finance Corporation must meet its energy standard, and the Municipality of Anchorage enforces adopted building codes. The Cold Climate Housing Research Center, now part of the National Renewable Energy Laboratory, publishes research on building in Alaska conditions.

Where spray foam earns its keep

  • Rim joists and band joists: a classic leak point where foam outperforms batts.
  • Crawlspace walls: closed-cell foam can insulate and air-seal foundation walls.
  • Cathedral and unvented roof assemblies: where there's no room for ventilation, closed-cell foam is a common approach, designed to code.
  • Cabins, shops and metal buildings: closed-cell foam reduces condensation on cold metal panels.
  • Air sealing at penetrations such as plumbing and wiring holes, often with small canned foams.

Cold weather and installation quality

Spray foam chemicals and the surfaces they're sprayed onto must be within the manufacturer's temperature and moisture limits. Cold or frosty substrates reduce adhesion and yield, and can cause shrinkage or pulling away from framing. Installers use heated hoses and drums, may heat the space, and some manufacturers offer formulations for colder conditions. Foam should be applied in lifts no thicker than the manufacturer allows, because overly thick passes can overheat.

Safety and code basics

  • Vacate during spraying. SPF releases chemicals, including isocyanates, during application. Occupants should stay out during installation and for the re-entry period the manufacturer specifies, commonly around a day. The U.S. EPA publishes homeowner guidance on spray foam.
  • Thermal barrier. Building codes generally require foam plastic in occupied spaces to be covered by an approved thermal barrier such as 1/2-inch gypsum board; some attics and crawlspaces allow an ignition barrier instead.
  • Combustion appliances. Tightening a house affects how furnaces, boilers, water heaters and woodstoves draft. Consider combustion safety testing and mechanical ventilation, often an HRV in Alaska homes.
  • Canned foam for DIY. One-component cans are fine for gaps and cracks. Two-component DIY kits for larger areas demand careful attention to protective gear and instructions.

Questions to ask an installer

  • Which product and type, what R-value per inch, and what total thickness?
  • How will vapor control and the thermal barrier be handled to meet code?
  • What temperature and moisture limits apply, and how will you meet them on my job?
  • Are you registered as a contractor in Alaska and insured, and have your sprayers been trained by the manufacturer or an industry program?
  • How long must we stay out of the house?

Frequently asked questions

Is closed-cell spray foam a vapor barrier?

At typical installed thicknesses, closed-cell foam acts as a vapor retarder with low permeability. Open-cell foam does not and usually needs a separate vapor retarder in cold climates.

Can spray foam be installed in winter in Alaska?

Yes, if the installer keeps chemicals and substrates within the manufacturer's limits, often by heating the work area. Frost or moisture on surfaces must be eliminated first.

How long should I stay out of my house after spray foam?

Follow the re-occupancy time specified by the foam manufacturer and installer, which is commonly around 24 hours, with ventilation during and after.
