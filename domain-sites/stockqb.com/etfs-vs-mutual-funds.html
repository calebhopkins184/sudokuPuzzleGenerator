ETFs vs mutual funds: the key differences explained

Updated November 2, 2026 · By the StockQB team

ETFs and mutual funds both pool investors' money into a basket of stocks, bonds, or other assets. The main difference is how you buy them: ETFs trade on an exchange throughout the day like a stock, while mutual fund orders execute once per day at the fund's net asset value (NAV) after the market closes.

Side-by-side comparison

FeatureETFMutual fund
How you buyOn an exchange through a brokerage, any time the market is openDirectly from the fund company or through a brokerage

Automatic investingSupported at some brokers, often with fractional sharesEasy to set fixed-dollar recurring purchases

Active or index?Both exist; most are index-basedBoth exist

Both are regulated investment products, and both can hold the same underlying investments. Many fund companies offer the same index strategy in both forms.

How trading works

ETFs: priced all day

An ETF's price changes throughout the trading day as investors buy and sell. You can use the same order types as for stocks — market, limit, stop. Because trades go through an exchange, there is a bid-ask spread; for large, popular ETFs it is often a penny, but for niche ETFs it can be wider.

An ETF's market price usually stays very close to the value of its holdings because large institutions called authorized participants can create or redeem ETF shares when prices drift. In stressed markets or for thinly traded funds, the price can trade at a small premium or discount to NAV.

Mutual funds: priced once a day

When you place a mutual fund order during the day, you do not know the exact price. All orders received before the cutoff — usually the 4:00 p.m. Eastern market close — receive that day's NAV. Orders after the cutoff get the next day's NAV.

This sounds like a disadvantage, but for long-term investors it rarely matters. Many investors like that it removes the temptation to trade on intraday moves.

Costs to compare

  • Expense ratio. The annual percentage of your investment the fund charges. This applies to both types and is usually the most important cost. Broad index funds in either wrapper can be very cheap.
  • Sales loads. Some mutual funds, typically sold through commissioned advisors, charge a front-end load when you buy or a back-end load when you sell. Many no-load funds exist.
  • 12b-1 fees. An ongoing marketing and distribution fee included in some mutual funds' expense ratios.
  • Transaction fees. Some brokerages charge to buy mutual funds from other fund families. Most major brokers do not charge commissions on ETFs.
  • Bid-ask spread. An implicit cost on every ETF trade.

The SEC's investor education site, Investor.gov, has a mutual fund and ETF cost calculator and glossary.

Taxes: why ETFs are often more efficient

In a taxable brokerage account, funds must pass along capital gains they realize when they sell holdings. Mutual funds that must sell securities to meet redemptions can end up distributing capital gains to everyone who owns the fund — even if you did not sell.

Most ETFs handle redemptions differently. Authorized participants exchange ETF shares for a basket of the underlying securities "in kind," which lets the fund move out low-cost-basis holdings without selling them. As a result, many stock ETFs distribute few or no capital gains.

This advantage disappears inside tax-advantaged accounts like IRAs and 401(k)s, where distributions are not taxed each year. Index mutual funds are also generally fairly tax-efficient because they trade less than active funds.

Which one should you choose?

An ETF may fit better if you:

  • Invest in a taxable brokerage account and want to minimize yearly tax distributions.
  • Want a low or no minimum to get started.
  • Want flexibility to use limit orders or trade at a specific time.
  • Plan to move between brokerages; ETFs can be held almost anywhere.

A mutual fund may fit better if you:

  • Want to invest a fixed dollar amount automatically every payday.
  • Invest mainly through a 401(k), where mutual funds and collective trusts are common.
  • Prefer not to see or react to intraday price swings.
  • Already use a fund company with low-cost index mutual funds and no minimums.

Common misconceptions

  • "ETFs are riskier than mutual funds." Risk comes from what the fund holds, not the wrapper. A broad stock index ETF and a broad stock index mutual fund carry similar market risk.
  • "All ETFs are index funds." Many are, but actively managed ETFs and specialized products — leveraged, inverse, and single-sector funds — also exist. Leveraged and inverse ETFs are designed for short holding periods and can behave unexpectedly over longer periods.
  • "Mutual funds are outdated." They remain the backbone of most workplace retirement plans and are perfectly suitable for long-term investing.

Bottom line

For a long-term investor, choosing the right investments and costs matters far more than choosing the wrapper. A low-cost, broadly diversified index fund is a strong core holding whether it is an ETF or a mutual fund. Put that core to work in the beginner portfolio playbook.
