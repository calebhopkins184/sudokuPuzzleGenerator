Stock order types: market, limit, stop, and stop-limit orders

Updated November 2, 2026 · By the StockQB team

A market order buys or sells immediately at the best available price but does not guarantee the price. A limit order guarantees the price — your maximum to buy or minimum to sell — but not that the order will fill. Stop orders wait until a trigger price is reached and then become market or limit orders.

Market orders

A market order says "fill this now." For a large, heavily traded stock or ETF during regular hours, a market order usually fills within a few cents of the quoted price — near the ask when buying and near the bid when selling (see how to read a stock quote).

Use caution with market orders:

  • At the open, when prices can swing as overnight news is absorbed.
  • On thinly traded stocks, where the spread is wide and there may be few shares at the quoted price.
  • During fast-moving news, when the price can move between your click and the fill.

Limit orders

A buy limit sets the maximum you will pay. A sell limit sets the minimum you will accept. Your order fills only at your limit price or better.

Example: XMPL (a hypothetical stock) trades at $48.62. You enter a buy limit at $48.00. If the price falls to $48.00 or lower and there are sellers, you may be filled at $48.00 or less. If XMPL climbs to $52, you never buy.

Limit orders are the safer choice when spreads are wide, when trading outside regular hours, or when buying less-liquid ETFs. A limit placed near the current ask works like a market order with a safety cap.

Note that a limit order is not guaranteed to fill even if the stock trades at your price, because other orders at that price may be ahead of yours.

Stop orders (stop-loss)

A sell stop sits below the current price. When the stock trades at or below your stop price, it becomes a market order to sell. Investors use it to limit losses or protect gains.

Example: you own XMPL at $50 and set a sell stop at $45. If XMPL trades down to $45, your order becomes a market sell and fills at the next available price.

The catch is gaps. If XMPL closes at $47 and bad news overnight makes it open at $40, your stop triggers at the open and sells near $40 — not $45. A stop order guarantees you get out, not the price you get out at.

A buy stop sits above the current price and triggers a market buy if the price rises to it. Traders use it to enter on breakouts or to limit losses on short positions.

Stop-limit orders

A stop-limit has two prices: the stop that triggers it and the limit that caps the fill price.

Example: sell stop at $45, limit at $44. If XMPL trades at $45, a sell limit at $44 goes live. You will sell at $44 or better — or not at all. In the overnight-gap example, with XMPL opening at $40, the order triggers but does not fill, and you still own the shares as they fall.

That is the trade-off: stop orders risk a bad price; stop-limit orders risk no fill.

Trailing stop orders

A trailing stop sets the stop a fixed dollar amount or percentage below the market's highest price since you placed the order. As the price rises, the stop rises with it; when the price falls, the stop stays put.

Example: a 10% trailing stop on a stock at $50 starts with a stop at $45. If the stock climbs to $60, the stop moves to $54. If the stock then falls to $54, the order triggers as a market sell. Trailing stops can lock in gains automatically but share the same gap risk as regular stops, and a tight trail can trigger on normal daily swings.

Other details beginners run into

  • Fractional shares. Many brokers only allow market orders for fractional shares, or execute them at set times.
  • Partial fills. A large limit order can fill in pieces over time. Some brokers charge per execution on certain products, so check fees.
  • Settlement. U.S. stock trades settle one business day after the trade date (T+1). Selling a stock and immediately using the proceeds in a cash account can be restricted until settlement — ask your broker how it handles unsettled funds.
  • Market hours. Regular hours on the NYSE and Nasdaq are 9:30 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. Eastern Time.

Which order should a long-term investor use?

For buying a broad, heavily traded ETF during regular hours to hold for years, a market order or a limit order set near the ask both work well. For anything thinly traded, during extended hours, or in a volatile market, use a limit order. Use stop orders deliberately, understanding that they can sell at a worse price than you expect.

The SEC and FINRA publish plain-language explainers on order types; Investor.gov is a good place to start.
