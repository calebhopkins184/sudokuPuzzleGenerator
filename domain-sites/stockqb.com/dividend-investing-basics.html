Dividend investing basics: yield, key dates, and taxes

Updated November 2, 2026 · By the StockQB team

A dividend is a cash payment a company makes to shareholders, usually from its profits. In the U.S., many companies pay quarterly. To receive a dividend you generally must own the shares before the ex-dividend date, and the dividend yield — annual dividends divided by share price — tells you the income you get relative to what you pay.

How dividends work

A company's board of directors decides whether to pay a dividend and how much. Companies that pay dividends are often established businesses with steady cash flow; fast-growing companies frequently reinvest profits instead.

Dividends are not guaranteed. A board can raise, cut, suspend, or eliminate a dividend at any time.

The four key dividend dates

DateWhat happens
Declaration dateThe board announces the dividend amount, record date, and payment date
Ex-dividend dateThe cutoff: you must own the shares before this date to receive the dividend
Record dateThe company checks its records to see who the shareholders are
Payment dateThe dividend is paid to eligible shareholders

Since U.S. stock trades moved to T+1 settlement (one business day) in May 2024, the ex-dividend date and record date usually fall on the same business day. The practical rule is simple: buy before the ex-dividend date if you want the upcoming payment. If you buy on or after the ex-date, the seller gets it.

On the ex-dividend date, the stock price typically drops by roughly the dividend amount, all else equal, because the company's cash is about to leave. That is why buying just before the ex-date to "capture" a dividend is not free money.

Dividend yield

Dividend yield = Annual dividends per share ÷ Share price

Example: a hypothetical stock trading at $50 pays $0.50 per quarter, or $2.00 per year.

Yield = $2.00 ÷ $50 = 4.0%

Yield moves inversely with price. If the stock falls to $40 and the dividend stays at $2.00, the yield rises to 5.0%. That is why an unusually high yield can be a warning sign rather than a bargain.

Payout ratio: can the dividend last?

Payout ratio = Dividends per share ÷ Earnings per share

If the company above earns $4.00 per share, its payout ratio is $2.00 ÷ $4.00 = 50%. It keeps half its profits for reinvestment, debt payments, or a cushion.

  • A moderate payout ratio leaves room to keep paying even if earnings dip.
  • A payout ratio near or above 100% means the company is paying out everything it earns — or more — which may not be sustainable.
  • Some business types, such as real estate investment trusts (REITs), routinely have high payout ratios because of how they are structured, so compare within an industry. Cash-flow-based payout ratios are often more informative for these.

Avoiding yield traps

A yield trap is a stock whose high yield reflects a falling price and a dividend that is likely to be cut. Warning signs:

  • Yield far above similar companies.
  • Payout ratio above 100% or rising quickly.
  • Falling revenue or earnings.
  • Heavy or rising debt.
  • A long slide in the share price.

A smaller dividend that grows reliably can be worth more over time than a large one that gets slashed.

Dividend growth investing

Some investors focus on companies with long records of raising dividends each year. The "S&P 500 Dividend Aristocrats," for example, are S&P 500 members that have increased dividends for at least 25 consecutive years. A history of raises signals durable cash flow and management commitment, but past increases do not guarantee future ones.

How dividends are taxed

In a taxable U.S. account, dividends are generally taxed in the year you receive them, even if you reinvest them. There are two main categories:

TypeTax treatment
Qualified dividendsTaxed at the lower long-term capital gains rates
Ordinary (nonqualified) dividendsTaxed at your regular income tax rate

To be qualified, a dividend generally must come from a U.S. company or a qualifying foreign company, and you must meet a holding period: own the stock for more than 60 days during the 121-day period that begins 60 days before the ex-dividend date. Most dividends from REITs and money market funds are ordinary dividends.

Your broker reports dividends on Form 1099-DIV. Inside an IRA or 401(k), dividends are not taxed each year. For the current rules, see IRS Publication 550, and consult a tax professional about your situation.

Reinvesting with a DRIP

A dividend reinvestment plan (DRIP) automatically uses your dividends to buy more shares, often including fractional shares, usually without commissions. Most brokerages let you turn reinvestment on for individual stocks and funds.

Reinvesting compounds your holdings: more shares produce more dividends, which buy more shares. If you need income — in retirement, for example — you can turn reinvestment off and receive cash instead.

Individual dividend stocks vs dividend funds

ApproachProsCons
Individual stocksControl over holdings; no fund expense ratioRequires research; concentration risk
Dividend ETFs or mutual fundsInstant diversification across many payersExpense ratio; you accept the fund's rules
Broad market index fundAlready includes most dividend payers; very low costLower overall yield

Remember that total return — price change plus dividends — is what builds wealth. A company that reinvests profits into growth can reward shareholders just as well as one that pays them out. A dividend focus can be one part of a diversified beginner portfolio, not the whole thing.
