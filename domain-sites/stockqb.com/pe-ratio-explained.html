P/E ratio explained: what it tells you about a stock's valuation

Updated November 2, 2026 · By the StockQB team

The price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio is a stock's share price divided by its earnings per share (EPS). It shows how much investors are paying for each dollar of a company's annual profit. A higher P/E usually means investors expect faster growth — or that the stock is expensive.

The P/E formula

P/E ratio = Share price ÷ Earnings per share (EPS)

Example: a hypothetical stock trades at $60 and earned $3.00 per share over the last twelve months.

P/E = $60 ÷ $3.00 = 20

Investors are paying $20 for every $1 of annual earnings. Another way to read it: if earnings stayed flat forever, it would take 20 years of profits to "earn back" the price.

Trailing vs forward P/E

TypeEarnings usedStrengthWeakness
Trailing P/E (TTM)Actual EPS over the past 12 monthsBased on reported resultsBackward-looking
Forward P/EAnalysts' estimated EPS for the next 12 months or fiscal yearReflects expected growthEstimates can be wrong

Using the example above, if analysts expect EPS of $3.50 next year, the forward P/E is $60 ÷ $3.50 ≈ 17.1. A forward P/E well below the trailing P/E implies the market expects earnings to grow.

Quote pages usually label which version they show. Make sure you compare like with like.

Earnings yield: P/E flipped over

The earnings yield is EPS divided by price — the inverse of P/E.

Earnings yield = EPS ÷ Price = 1 ÷ P/E

A P/E of 20 equals an earnings yield of 5%. Some investors compare earnings yield with bond yields to judge whether stocks look expensive relative to safer alternatives. It is a rough comparison, because earnings are not paid out like bond interest and can grow or shrink.

What is a "good" P/E?

There is no universal good P/E. It depends on the company's growth, profitability, risk, and industry — and on interest rates.

  • Fast-growing companies often carry high P/Es because investors expect earnings to rise sharply.
  • Mature, slow-growing companies — utilities, for example — often trade at lower P/Es.
  • Cyclical companies (automakers, steel, airlines) can look cheapest at the top of a cycle, when earnings are temporarily high, and most expensive at the bottom, when earnings collapse.

The most useful comparisons are:

  1. Against close competitors in the same industry.
  2. Against the company's own history.
  3. Against the broad market, such as the S&P 500's overall P/E.

The PEG ratio: adding growth

The PEG ratio divides the P/E by the expected annual earnings growth rate (as a whole number).

PEG = P/E ÷ Expected annual EPS growth (%)

Example: a P/E of 20 with expected growth of 15% a year gives a PEG of 20 ÷ 15 ≈ 1.33. A company with a P/E of 30 growing 30% a year has a PEG of 1.0.

Some investors view a PEG near or below 1 as reasonable, but the ratio is only as good as the growth estimate, which is a forecast.

CAPE: smoothing out the business cycle

The cyclically adjusted P/E (CAPE), popularized by economist Robert Shiller, divides price by average inflation-adjusted earnings over the previous ten years. It is usually applied to whole markets, like the S&P 500, rather than single stocks. Because it smooths out booms and busts, it is used to judge whether the market as a whole looks expensive by historical standards. It has been a poor short-term timing tool, but it has shown some relationship to long-run returns.

When P/E breaks down

  • Negative or zero earnings. If a company lost money, P/E is not meaningful and quote pages typically show "N/A" or a dash.
  • One-time items. A big asset sale can inflate earnings and make P/E look low; a one-time write-down can do the opposite. Look for "adjusted" or "operating" earnings and read what was excluded.
  • Different accounting. Share buybacks reduce share count and raise EPS without the business growing. Comparing companies across countries with different accounting standards adds noise.
  • Debt is ignored. Two companies with the same P/E can carry very different debt loads. Measures such as EV/EBITDA, which use enterprise value (market cap plus debt minus cash), account for this.

Other valuation ratios to know

RatioFormulaOften used for
Price-to-sales (P/S)Market cap ÷ annual revenueCompanies with little or no profit yet
Price-to-book (P/B)Price ÷ book value per shareBanks, insurers, asset-heavy businesses
EV/EBITDAEnterprise value ÷ earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortizationComparing companies with different debt levels
Dividend yieldAnnual dividend ÷ priceIncome-focused investing

How to use P/E sensibly

Use P/E as a starting question, not an answer. A low P/E asks: why is the market pessimistic, and is it wrong? A high P/E asks: what growth is priced in, and is it realistic? Answering those questions requires looking at revenue trends, profit margins, debt, and competition.
