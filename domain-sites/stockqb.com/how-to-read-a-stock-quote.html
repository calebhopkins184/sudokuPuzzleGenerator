How to read a stock quote: every field explained

Updated November 2, 2026 · By the StockQB team

A stock quote is a snapshot of how a stock is trading right now. At minimum it shows the ticker symbol, the last trade price, and the change for the day. Fuller quote pages add the bid and ask, volume, day and 52-week ranges, market capitalization, P/E ratio, earnings per share, and dividend information.

A sample quote, field by field

Here is a quote for a hypothetical company, Example Corp, with the made-up ticker XMPL. Real quote pages on brokerage and financial sites look very similar.

Example Corp (XMPL)   48.62 +0.74 (+1.55%)
Bid 48.61 x 300  |  Ask 48.63 x 500
Open 47.95  |  Prev close 47.88  |  Day range 47.80 – 48.90
52-wk range 36.10 – 55.40  |  Volume 3.2M  |  Avg vol 4.1M
Mkt cap 24.3B  |  P/E (TTM) 18.4  |  EPS (TTM) 2.64
Fwd div & yield 1.20 (2.47%)  |  Ex-div date (shown on quote page)  |  Beta 1.08

Price fields

FieldWhat it means
TickerThe short code used to trade the stock on an exchange, such as XMPL
Last priceThe price of the most recent trade (48.62)
ChangeHow far the price has moved from the previous close, in dollars and percent (+0.74, +1.55%)
Previous closeThe last price at the end of the prior regular session (47.88)
OpenThe first trade price of the current regular session
Day rangeThe lowest and highest prices traded today
52-week rangeThe lowest and highest prices over the past year

The change is measured from the previous close, not from the open. That is why a stock can be "up" for the day even if it has fallen since the opening bell.

Many free websites show quotes delayed by about 15 to 20 minutes. Brokerage platforms generally show real-time quotes to account holders. Check for a "real-time" or "delayed" label before acting on a price.

Bid, ask, and the spread

The bid is the highest price buyers are currently offering. The ask (or offer) is the lowest price sellers are currently willing to accept. The numbers after the "x" show how many shares are available at those prices — sometimes displayed in round lots of 100 shares, so check your platform's convention.

The bid-ask spread is the gap between them. In the example, the spread is 2 cents (48.63 − 48.61). Large, heavily traded companies often have spreads of a penny or two; thinly traded stocks can have much wider spreads, which is a hidden cost every time you buy and sell.

If you place a market order to buy, you will typically pay near the ask. A market order to sell typically fills near the bid.

Volume and liquidity

Volume is the number of shares traded so far today. Average volume is the typical daily volume over a recent period, often three months. Comparing the two tells you whether today is unusually busy: volume far above average often accompanies news such as earnings reports.

Higher volume generally means better liquidity — you can buy or sell without moving the price much, and spreads tend to be tighter.

Valuation fields

FieldWhat it means
Market capShare price × shares outstanding: the total market value of the company's stock
EPS (TTM)Earnings per share over the trailing twelve months
P/E (TTM)Price divided by trailing EPS — how much investors pay per dollar of earnings
BetaHow much the stock has historically moved relative to the overall market; 1.0 means in line with the market

Market cap is how investors size companies. Rough, commonly used labels are large-cap (around $10 billion and up), mid-cap (roughly $2 billion to $10 billion), and small-cap (below that), though exact cutoffs vary by source.

To learn what the P/E figure tells you — and what it does not — see the P/E ratio guide.

Dividend fields

  • Dividend (annual or forward): the yearly cash payment per share, often based on the latest quarterly rate × 4.
  • Yield: the annual dividend divided by the share price. In the example, 1.20 ÷ 48.62 ≈ 2.47%.
  • Ex-dividend date: you generally need to own the shares before this date to receive the next dividend.

Our dividend investing guide explains the full dividend calendar.

Trading-hours details

Regular trading on the NYSE and Nasdaq runs from 9:30 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. Eastern Time. Many quote pages separately show pre-market and after-hours prices. Extended-hours trading usually has lower volume and wider spreads, and many brokers only accept limit orders during those sessions. See stock order types for why that matters.

Quick reading routine

When you pull up any quote, scan it in this order:

  1. Last price and change — what is happening today?
  2. 52-week range — is the price near a yearly high or low?
  3. Volume vs average — is something unusual going on?
  4. Bid-ask spread — how costly is it to trade?
  5. Market cap and P/E — how big is the company, and how richly is it valued?
  6. Dividend and yield — does it pay shareholders cash?

A quote tells you what the market thinks right now. It does not tell you whether the business is good. That requires looking at the company's financial statements and competitive position — or owning a diversified fund instead.
