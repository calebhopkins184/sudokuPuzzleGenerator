Cost per use: how to calculate what something really costs

Cost per use is the total cost of owning something divided by the number of times you actually use it. It's the clearest way to compare a cheap item you'll replace often with a durable one that lasts years, as long as you're honest about how often you'll use it.

Updated November 2, 2026 · By the High Qulity team

The formula

Cost per use = (purchase price + upkeep − resale value) ÷ number of uses

  • Purchase price: what you pay, including tax and shipping.
  • Upkeep: repairs, maintenance, and consumables over the item's life (resoles, sharpening, cleaning, replacement parts).
  • Resale value: what you could reasonably sell it for when you're done. Durable goods from respected makers often keep some value; disposable ones rarely do.
  • Number of uses: how many times you'll realistically use it over its life. This is the number people inflate, so be honest.

Worked examples

The numbers below are hypothetical, chosen to show how the math works. They are not price quotes; plug in real prices from where you shop.

Example 1: everyday boots

Expected life1 year8 years
Upkeep$0$150 (two hypothetical resoles plus care)
Resale$0$0 (assume worn out)
Uses (150 days/year)1501,200

The durable boots cost five times as much up front but come out slightly cheaper per wear, and you'd have bought eight pairs of the cheap ones over the same period. If the welted boots last longer than eight years, the gap widens. If you only wear them 30 days a year, the cheap boots may never wear out, and the math flips.

Example 3: the trendy jacket

A hypothetical $250 jacket bought for one season and worn 10 times costs $25 per wear. A $120 classic jacket worn 40 times a year for five years costs $0.60 per wear. Style longevity matters as much as material durability: a well-made item you stop wearing has the same cost per use as a badly made one.

The traps

Cost per use is easy to abuse. Watch for these:

  1. Overestimating uses. The expensive espresso machine, the premium running shoes, the high-end tool you need twice a year. Base the estimate on how you've actually behaved, not on who you hope to become.
  2. Ignoring upfront cost. An item with great cost per use is still a bad buy if it means carrying credit card debt. Interest can wipe out any savings.
  3. Assuming expensive means durable. Price is not proof. Use the construction checks in our clothing, furniture, and tools guides to confirm the item will actually last.
  4. Forgetting fit and needs change. Kids grow, bodies change, homes get smaller. A durable item that no longer fits your life has stopped generating uses.
  5. Leaving out upkeep. Some durable items are cheap to buy and expensive to maintain. Include the real maintenance cost.

When buying cheap is the smart move

  • You're not sure you'll use it. Buy an inexpensive or secondhand version first, then upgrade if it earns its place.
  • The item is consumable by nature, such as running shoe foam, nonstick coatings, sandpaper, and printer ink.
  • Technology moves fast. Phones and laptops become obsolete before they wear out, so durability matters less than value and support length.
  • It's for a one-off job. Borrow, rent, or buy basic.

A simple decision checklist

  1. How many times will I realistically use this per year, based on past behavior?
  2. How long will each option last, and can it be repaired?
  3. What upkeep will each need?
  4. Will it hold any resale value?
  5. Can I afford the better option without debt?
  6. Does the construction actually support the claimed lifespan?

If the durable option wins on cost per use and you can pay for it comfortably, it's usually the better buy. If not, buy the cheaper option without guilt.

Frequently asked questions

Does a low cost per use mean I should buy the expensive version?

Only if you will really use it that often and keep it that long, and if you can afford the upfront cost without straining your budget. Cost per use is a comparison tool, not permission to overspend.

Should I include repair costs?

Yes. Resoling, sharpening, re-upholstering, and replacement parts are part of the true cost. Durable items often need some upkeep, and including it keeps the comparison fair.

What if I don't know how long something will last?

Use a conservative estimate, such as the warranty period or the low end of what owners typically report, and check whether the item is repairable. Repairability is a useful hedge when lifespan is uncertain.
