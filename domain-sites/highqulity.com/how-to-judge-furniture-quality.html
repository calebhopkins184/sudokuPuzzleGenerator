How to judge furniture quality: materials, joinery, and upholstery

Well-built furniture uses stable materials (solid wood or quality plywood), strong joints such as dovetails and mortise-and-tenon, and drawers and frames built to take decades of use. The fastest checks are to pull out a drawer, look underneath, and give the piece a firm push to see if it wobbles.

Updated November 2, 2026 · By the High Qulity team

Materials: what it's really made of

MaterialDurabilityNotes
Solid hardwood (oak, maple, walnut, cherry)ExcellentCan be refinished many times; moves slightly with humidity, so good construction allows for that
Solid softwood (pine)GoodDents more easily; fine for rustic or painted pieces
Veneer over plywoodVery goodStable and strong; thin veneer limits how much it can be refinished
MDFFairSmooth and good for painted surfaces; heavy, sags under long spans, swells if wet
Particleboard with laminateLowTypical of flat-pack; holds screws poorly and is hard to repair once damaged

Labels can mislead. "Solid wood construction" might apply only to the frame, and "wood" can include engineered wood. Ask specifically what the top, sides, drawer boxes, and back are made of.

Joinery: how the pieces connect

Joints are where furniture fails. Strong joints interlock wood so glue isn't doing all the work.

  • Dovetail joints: interlocking fan-shaped pins and tails, the classic sign of a quality drawer. Hand-cut dovetails are uneven and narrow; machine-cut ones are uniform. Both are strong.
  • Mortise and tenon: a tongue (tenon) glued into a slot (mortise). The standard for strong chair, table, and bed frames.
  • Dowels: wooden pins glued into matching holes. Can be quite strong when well made.
  • Corner blocks: small blocks glued and screwed inside the corners of tables and chairs where legs meet the apron. They add rigidity and are a good sign.
  • Tongue and groove: used for panels and tops; allows for wood movement.
  • Cam locks, staples, and glue only: typical of flat-pack furniture. Fine for light use, but they loosen when the piece is moved or reassembled.

Drawers tell you almost everything

Pull a drawer all the way out and look closely. Makers who build good drawers rarely cut corners elsewhere.

  • Joints: dovetails at the front and back corners are best. Stapled or glued butt joints are a budget sign.
  • Drawer bottom: it should sit in grooves cut into the sides (dadoes), not be stapled to the underside. Plywood bottoms are better than thin hardboard.
  • Slides: full-extension ball-bearing slides run smoothly under load. Traditional wood-on-wood runners also last if they're waxed and well fitted. Flimsy plastic rollers wear out.
  • Stops: a good drawer shouldn't fall out when fully opened.
  • Dust panels between drawers in dressers are a mark of careful construction.

Case goods: quick checks

  1. The wobble test. Push firmly on a top corner. Good pieces stay rigid.
  2. Look underneath and behind. Hidden surfaces should be reasonably finished. A back panel set into grooves or screwed in is better than thin board stapled on.
  3. Doors. They should hang evenly with consistent gaps and close without rubbing. Look for sturdy hinges with multiple screws.
  4. Weight. Heavier usually means solid wood or plywood rather than hollow-core panels, although MDF is heavy too.
  5. Finish. Even color, no drips, and no rough spots. Run your hand along edges and the undersides of tabletops.

Sofas and upholstered chairs

With upholstery, the important parts are hidden, so ask questions and test hard.

Frame

  • Kiln-dried hardwood frames resist warping and cracking. Joints that are glued, doweled, and reinforced with corner blocks last longest.
  • Avoid frames of particleboard or soft, wet wood that can twist over time.
  • Lift one front corner a few inches off the floor. If the other front leg lifts quickly too, the frame is rigid. If it twists, it's weak.

Cushions and fabric

  • Higher-density foam lasts longer; foam wrapped in a layer of down or fiber is softer on top but needs fluffing.
  • Removable, zippered cushion covers let you flip, clean, or replace them.
  • Upholstery fabric durability is often rated by abrasion tests (such as "double rubs"). Ask for the rating if you have pets or kids.
  • Pattern matching, straight seams, and evenly spaced tufting signal careful work.

Secondhand is a quality shortcut

Older solid wood furniture is often better built than new pieces at the same price, and it has already proven it lasts. At estate sales and thrift stores, use the same checks: pull the drawers, look for dovetails, push on corners, and look underneath. Surface scratches and dated finishes are easy to fix. Cracked frames, water-swollen particleboard, and pest damage are not.

Frequently asked questions

Is veneer furniture bad?

No. Veneer over a stable plywood core can be excellent and resists warping better than some solid wood. Veneer over particleboard is more of a concern because the core is weak, swells with moisture, and holds screws poorly. What's under the veneer matters most.

How can I tell solid wood from veneer?

Look at an edge or the end of a tabletop. On solid wood, the grain pattern continues around the edge and the end grain is visible. On veneer, the edge often shows a thin band or a repeating grain that doesn't match the top.

What foam density is good for sofa cushions?

Higher-density foam generally lasts longer. Seat cushions are often made with foam around 1.8 pounds per cubic foot or more, and high-resilience foam with even higher density holds its shape longer. Ask the retailer for the specification.
