Cast iron vs stainless steel vs carbon steel: cookware that lasts

If you want pans that last decades, choose from three metals: cast iron, stainless steel (ideally fully clad), and carbon steel. All three can outlive their owners with basic care. Nonstick pans, by contrast, are consumables: their coatings wear out in a few years no matter how much you paid.

Cast iron

Cast iron is poured into a mold as a single piece, so there are no coatings to wear off and nothing to come loose. A well-kept skillet can be passed down for generations, and old pans are commonly restored and reused.

Signs of a good cast iron pan

  • Flat base. Set it on a flat counter and press the edges. It shouldn't rock. A warped pan heats poorly, especially on glass and induction cooktops.
  • Even wall thickness with no visible cracks or pits deep in the cooking surface.
  • Surface finish. Many vintage pans were machined smooth; most modern ones have a pebbly cast texture. Both work fine. A smoother surface becomes nonstick faster, and rough pans can be sanded smooth if you like.
  • Helper handle on larger pans, because a 12-inch skillet is heavy.

Enameled cast iron

Enameled cast iron, as in Dutch ovens, adds a glass coating that doesn't need seasoning and handles acidic braises well. It lasts a very long time but the enamel can chip if dropped or crack from thermal shock. Look for even enamel with no bubbles or thin spots, and a lid that fits snugly.

Care basics

  • Wash with hot water and a brush or scraper. A little dish soap is fine on a seasoned pan.
  • Dry immediately, then heat briefly on the stove to drive off moisture.
  • Wipe a very thin film of oil on the surface while warm.
  • Don't soak it or put it in the dishwasher.

Stainless steel

Stainless steel is the workhorse of professional kitchens: non-reactive, dishwasher-safe, and nearly indestructible. On its own, though, stainless conducts heat poorly, so good stainless pans bond it to a core of aluminum or copper.

Clad vs disc-bottom

  • Fully clad (tri-ply or multi-ply): layers of stainless and aluminum (or copper) run across the bottom and up the sides. Heat spreads evenly, which matters for skillets and sauté pans.
  • Disc-bottom (impact-bonded): a thick disc of aluminum is attached only to the base. It's fine for stockpots and saucepans that mostly hold liquid, but sides can run cooler and food can scorch at the edge of the disc.

Reading the numbers

You'll see grades like 18/10, 18/8, and 18/0. The numbers refer to approximate chromium and nickel content. Nickel improves shine and corrosion resistance; 18/0 has little nickel, which makes it magnetic and therefore useful on the outer layer of induction-compatible pans. Many quality pans use 18/10 inside and a magnetic stainless outside.

Signs of a well-made stainless pan

  • Noticeable heft for its size; very thin pans warp and develop hot spots.
  • Handles firmly riveted or welded, with no wobble. Rivets are very durable; welded handles are easier to clean.
  • A rolled or flared rim for drip-free pouring.
  • Oven-safe handles (no plastic) so the pan can go from stovetop to oven.

To prevent sticking, preheat the pan before adding oil, then let food release naturally before flipping. Bar Keepers Friend or a similar cleanser removes discoloration.

Carbon steel

Carbon steel behaves much like cast iron, since it's mostly iron and needs seasoning, but it's rolled into thinner sheets. That makes it lighter, quicker to heat, and smoother out of the box. It's the traditional material for woks, crêpe pans, and the French-style skillets used in many restaurant kitchens.

  • Look for a thick gauge (heavier pans resist warping) and a riveted or one-piece handle.
  • New pans often arrive with a protective wax or oil coating that should be scrubbed off before seasoning; follow the maker's instructions.
  • Expect the color to turn patchy and dark as seasoning builds. That's normal.
  • Care is the same as cast iron: no soaking, dry immediately, and keep lightly oiled.

What about nonstick?

Nonstick pans are useful for eggs and delicate fish, but every nonstick coating, whether PTFE-based or "ceramic", wears out with use, heat, and scrubbing. Treat them as a replaceable item and don't pay a buy-it-for-life premium for them. To make one last longer, cook on low to medium heat, use wooden or silicone utensils, avoid the dishwasher, and stop using it once the coating is visibly flaking. A well-seasoned carbon steel pan can cover many of the same jobs indefinitely.

Copper and aluminum, briefly

  • Copper conducts heat superbly and lasts generations, but it's expensive and must be lined (traditionally tin, now usually stainless) because copper reacts with food. Tin linings can be re-tinned by specialists.
  • Plain aluminum is light and heats evenly but dents, reacts with acidic foods, and is mainly used in sheet pans and restaurant sauté pans. Hard-anodized aluminum is tougher and less reactive.

A starter set that lasts

You don't need a 14-piece set. Most home cooks are well served by:

  1. A 10 to 12 inch cast iron or carbon steel skillet
  2. A 10 to 12 inch fully clad stainless skillet or sauté pan
  3. A 2 to 3 quart stainless saucepan with lid
  4. A large stockpot (disc-bottom is fine)
  5. An enameled cast iron Dutch oven
  6. An inexpensive nonstick skillet for eggs, replaced as needed

Buying pieces individually lets you choose the right material for each job instead of paying for pans you'll never use.

Frequently asked questions

Can you ruin a cast iron pan?

Rust, burnt-on residue, and stripped seasoning can almost always be fixed by scrubbing and re-seasoning. Cast iron can crack from severe thermal shock or a hard drop, and a badly warped pan may not sit flat, but those are uncommon with normal use.

Is fully clad stainless worth it over disc-bottom?

For skillets and sauté pans, usually yes, because the aluminum or copper layer runs up the sides and heats more evenly. For stockpots that mostly boil liquid, a good disc-bottom pot works well and costs less.

Which cookware works on induction?

Cast iron, carbon steel, and magnetic stainless steel work on induction. Test with a magnet: if it sticks firmly to the base, the pan will work. Aluminum, copper, and some stainless lines without a magnetic layer won't.
