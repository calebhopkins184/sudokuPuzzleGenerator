Clothing

How to tell quality clothing: fabric, stitching, and construction

Quality clothing shows itself in three places: the fabric (fiber, weight, and density), the stitching (small, even, and secure), and the finishing details that take extra time, such as matched patterns, clean seams, and well-sewn buttons. You can check all three in about two minutes without reading the brand tag.

Updated November 2, 2026 · By the High Qulity team

Start with the fabric

Fabric is where most of a garment's lifespan is decided. Even perfect sewing can't save thin, loosely woven cloth made from short fibers.

Read the fiber content label

In the U.S., clothing labels must list fiber content by percentage. That label is your first clue.

FiberStrengthsWatch out for
CottonBreathable, easy care, ages wellShort-staple cotton pills and thins; long-staple types such as Pima and Egyptian are smoother and stronger
Wool (incl. merino)Warm, odor-resistant, resilientVery loosely knit wool pills and stretches; needs gentle washing
LinenVery strong, cool, softens with ageWrinkles easily (that's normal, not a defect)
Polyester / nylonDurable, quick-drying, good in blends for workwearCheap versions pill, trap odor, and feel plasticky
Viscose / rayonSoft drape, comfortableWeak when wet and prone to shrinking unless well made
Elastane (spandex)Stretch and recovery in small amountsDegrades with heat and age; high percentages wear out sooner

Check weight and density

  • Hold it up to the light. On a shirt or knit, a lot of light passing through means a loose weave or knit that will wear thin.
  • Feel the weight. For denim, sweatshirts, and outerwear, heavier fabric generally lasts longer. Denim is often described in ounces per square yard, and T-shirts and knits in grams per square meter (GSM); higher numbers mean heavier cloth.
  • Do the stretch test. Gently stretch a small section, then let go. Good knits and stretch fabrics snap back. Fabric that stays rippled will bag out quickly.
  • Rub the surface. Rub two layers together for a few seconds. If you already see fuzz forming, expect pilling.

Examine the stitching

Stitching holds everything together, and it's the easiest place for a factory to save time.

  • Stitch density. Count stitches across one inch of a visible seam. More, smaller stitches are stronger and look cleaner. Long, loose stitches can snag and pull.
  • Straightness and evenness. Wavy lines, skipped stitches, and loose threads point to rushed work.
  • Thread tails and backtacking. Seams should be secured at the ends. Pull very gently on a loose thread end: if the seam starts unraveling, put it back.
  • Reinforcement at stress points. Look for bar tacks (short, dense zig-zag stitches) at pocket corners, belt loops, and fly openings on pants and workwear.

Look inside: seam finishes

Turn the garment inside out. The inside tells you how much care went into it.

Seam typeWhat it looks likeWhat it says
Flat-felledRaw edges folded under and stitched flat, two rows of stitching visible outsideVery durable; traditional on jeans, work shirts, and quality dress shirts
French seamRaw edges enclosed inside a narrow tube of fabricClean and durable; common on fine blouses and lightweight fabrics
Bound or tapedEdges wrapped in a strip of fabricExtra care, often in unlined jackets
Overlocked (serged)Edges wrapped by a looping threadStandard and fine for knits; on wovens it's the economical option
Raw / pinkedCut edges left exposedFraying risk unless the garment is lined

Also check the seam allowance (the fabric beyond the stitching) and the hem. Generous allowances and deep hems leave room for a tailor to let out or lengthen the garment later. Tiny allowances mean what you buy is what you get.

Finishing details that take time

  • Pattern matching. On stripes and plaids, check that the pattern lines up at the front placket, pockets, and side seams. Matching wastes fabric and takes skill, so budget makers skip it.
  • Buttons. They should be firmly attached, ideally with a thread shank (a short wrapped stem) on coats so they sit properly over thick fabric. Spare buttons are a good sign.
  • Buttonholes. Dense, clean stitching with no fraying threads. A sloppy buttonhole will fray first.
  • Zippers. Metal or sturdy coil zippers that run smoothly, sewn in flat with no puckering.
  • Collars and cuffs. Crisp and symmetrical, with the collar points matching each other.
  • Linings. In jackets and trousers, a lining should be sewn neatly with a little ease (a small pleat) so it doesn't pull when you move.

Knitwear specifics

  • Fully fashioned knitwear is shaped on the machine, with visible marks where stitches were increased or decreased near the seams. It holds its shape better than pieces cut from a knitted sheet and sewn together.
  • Linked seams join knit pieces loop to loop, making them flatter and stretchier than overlocked seams.
  • Ribbing at cuffs and hems should spring back firmly when stretched.

The two-minute fitting-room inspection

  1. Read the fiber label.
  2. Hold the fabric up to the light, then stretch and release a small section.
  3. Count stitches on a visible seam and look for loose threads.
  4. Turn it inside out and check the seam finishes and hem depth.
  5. Check buttons, buttonholes, and zipper.
  6. Look for pattern matching on stripes or plaids.
  7. Move in it: reach forward, sit, raise your arms. Good fit is part of quality, because ill-fitting clothes strain seams and stay in the closet.

Making good clothes last

Quality only pays off if you care for it. Wash less often and in cold water, skip the dryer for knits and anything with elastane, fold sweaters instead of hanging them, and fix small problems such as a loose button or a split seam right away. A basic sewing kit and a good local tailor will add years to your wardrobe.

Frequently asked questions

Is 100% cotton always better than a blend?

Not always. A small amount of elastane adds comfort and shape retention to jeans and knits, and polyester blends can improve durability in workwear. The issue is cheap blends used to cut costs, which tend to pill and lose shape. Judge the fabric by feel, density, and recovery, not only the label.

How many stitches per inch should a good shirt have?

As a rough guide, mass-market garments often have around 8 to 10 stitches per inch, while well-made shirts commonly have 12 or more, and fine dress shirts often go higher. Even, straight, tight stitching matters as much as the count.

Does thick fabric mean higher quality?

Weight helps durability in items like jeans, sweatshirts, and outerwear, but it isn't everything. A dense, tightly woven lightweight fabric can outlast a thick, loosely woven one. Hold it to the light and check how much shows through.
