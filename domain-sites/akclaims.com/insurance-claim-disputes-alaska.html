Disputes

Insurance Claim Disputes and the Alaska Division of Insurance

Last updated November 2, 2026

Disclaimer: AK Claims provides general educational information only. It is not legal, financial or insurance advice, and AK Claims is not an insurance company, adjuster or law firm. Your policy language controls. For advice about your situation, consult a licensed Alaska attorney, a licensed adjuster or the Alaska Division of Insurance.

If you disagree with your insurer about whether a loss is covered or how much it's worth, you have options. Start by getting the decision in writing and asking for the policy language behind it. From there, you can request reconsideration, use the policy's appraisal process for valuation disputes, file a complaint with the Alaska Division of Insurance, or get help from a licensed public adjuster or attorney.

What can I do if my insurance claim is denied in Alaska?

Ask for the denial or partial denial in writing with the specific policy provisions cited, gather evidence that addresses those reasons, submit a written request for reconsideration, and, if needed, contact the Alaska Division of Insurance or consult a licensed attorney. Watch the deadlines in your policy.

Step 1: Understand the decision

  • Request a written explanation identifying the policy language relied on.
  • Request a complete copy of your policy, including endorsements in effect on the date of loss.
  • Request the adjuster's estimate and any engineering or expert reports used.
  • Identify the type of dispute: coverage (whether the loss is covered at all) or amount (how much the covered loss is worth). Different tools fit each.

Step 2: Request reconsideration in writing

Write a clear, factual letter or email that identifies the claim number, explains what you disagree with and why, and includes supporting evidence such as contractor estimates, engineering reports, photos and receipts. Keep a copy and note the date sent. See how to document property damage.

Step 3: Consider appraisal for valuation disputes

Many property policies include an appraisal clause for disagreements about the amount of a loss. Each side picks an appraiser, the two appraisers choose an umpire, and an agreement by any two of them sets the amount. Appraisal generally doesn't decide whether something is covered.

Each side usually pays its own appraiser and splits the umpire's fee. Read your policy's appraisal provision carefully, as procedures differ.

Step 4: Contact the Alaska Division of Insurance

The Alaska Division of Insurance, part of the Department of Commerce, Community, and Economic Development, regulates insurers and licensed insurance professionals in the state. Its consumer services staff accept complaints and can contact the insurer about how a claim has been handled.

  • What the Division can do: Review whether the insurer followed Alaska insurance law and regulations, including the unfair claim settlement practices rules (AS 21.36.125 and 3 AAC 26); request a response from the insurer; and take regulatory action where violations are found.
  • What it generally can't do: Act as your lawyer, decide factual disputes the way a court would, or order a specific payment amount in a disputed valuation.
  • How to file: Visit the Division's official website for its consumer complaint process and current contact information. Include your claim number, policy number, a timeline and copies of key documents.

The Division's website also lets you look up whether an insurer, agent or adjuster is licensed in Alaska.

Deadlines you must not miss

Policies commonly include a "suit against us" provision limiting how long you have to sue the insurer, and state statutes of limitation also apply. These deadlines can run even while you're negotiating, so talk with an attorney before any deadline approaches.

  • Proof of loss deadlines after the insurer requests one.
  • Deadlines to complete repairs and claim withheld depreciation.
  • Policy and statutory deadlines to file a lawsuit.

Bad faith

Alaska courts recognize claims against insurers that unreasonably deny or delay payment of valid claims, commonly called bad faith. Whether conduct rises to that level is a legal question that depends on the facts. An Alaska attorney can evaluate whether it may apply.

Tips for productive disputes

  • Keep communication in writing, or confirm phone calls by email.
  • Stick to facts, documents and policy language.
  • Respond to requests for information promptly and keep copies.
  • Escalate to a supervisor or claims manager if an adjuster is unresponsive.
  • Keep a timeline of every event, contact and document.

Frequently asked questions

Can the Alaska Division of Insurance make my insurer pay?

The Division regulates insurers and investigates complaints about claim handling, but it generally doesn't act as your attorney or decide disputed claim values. It can require insurer responses and take regulatory action for violations.

What is the appraisal clause in an insurance policy?

It's a process in many property policies for resolving disagreements about the amount of a loss using appraisers chosen by each side and an umpire. It usually doesn't resolve coverage disputes.

Do I need a lawyer to dispute a claim?

Not always. Many disputes are resolved with better documentation or appraisal. Consider a licensed Alaska attorney for large losses, coverage denials, injuries, or when deadlines are approaching.
