Homeowners Insurance Claims in Alaska: A Step-by-Step Guide

Last updated November 2, 2026

Disclaimer: AK Claims provides general educational information only. It is not legal, financial or insurance advice, and AK Claims is not an insurance company, adjuster or law firm. Your policy language controls. For advice about your situation, consult a licensed Alaska attorney, a licensed adjuster or the Alaska Division of Insurance.

A homeowners insurance claim follows a predictable path: report the loss, protect the property from further damage, document everything, meet with the adjuster, and negotiate the settlement. Knowing how your policy is structured and what you're expected to do makes each step smoother.

What does a typical Alaska homeowners policy cover?

Most homeowners policies sold in Alaska follow a standard structure with several coverage parts: the house itself, other structures, personal belongings, additional living expenses, liability and medical payments to others. What's covered depends on the policy form, endorsements and exclusions in your specific policy.

Coverage partWhat it generally covers
Dwelling (often Coverage A)The house and attached structures such as an attached garage
Other structures (B)Detached garages, sheds, fences
Personal property (C)Belongings such as furniture, clothing and electronics, often with special limits on items like jewelry, firearms and cash
Loss of use / additional living expense (D)Extra costs of living elsewhere while the home is being repaired after a covered loss
Personal liability (E)Claims by others for injury or property damage you're legally responsible for
Medical payments (F)Small medical bills for guests injured on your property, regardless of fault

Common exclusions in standard policies include flood, earthquake and earth movement, wear and tear, gradual leaks, mold (often limited), and lack of maintenance. Endorsements can add coverage, such as earthquake or water backup. Read your declarations page and endorsements, not just the policy booklet.

Replacement cost vs. actual cash value

Replacement cost coverage pays to repair or replace with new materials of like kind and quality. Actual cash value (ACV) pays replacement cost minus depreciation. Many replacement cost policies first pay ACV, then pay the withheld depreciation after you complete repairs.

Check your policy for deadlines to complete repairs and claim the withheld amount (often called recoverable depreciation), and keep invoices and receipts to prove the work was done.

Step by step: filing a homeowners claim

  1. Make it safe. Get people out of danger. For water, shut off the main valve. For fire, gas or electrical hazards, call emergency services and utilities first.
  2. Notify your insurer promptly. Policies require prompt notice. Write down your claim number, the name of everyone you speak with, and what they said.
  3. Prevent further damage. Policies require reasonable steps to protect the property, such as tarping a roof, shutting off water or hiring water mitigation. Keep receipts; reasonable costs are usually part of the claim.
  4. Document before cleanup. Photograph and video everything. See how to document property damage.
  5. Get a full copy of your policy. Ask the insurer for the complete policy including declarations and all endorsements in effect on the date of loss.
  6. Meet the adjuster. Walk through the damage together. Point out everything, including hidden damage behind walls or under floors. Take your own notes.
  7. Get your own estimates. Obtain repair estimates from qualified, registered Alaska contractors so you can compare them with the adjuster's estimate.
  8. Submit proof of loss if requested. Some insurers require a sworn proof of loss within a set number of days after they request it. Calendar that deadline.
  9. Review the settlement. Compare the insurer's estimate line by line with your contractor's estimate. Ask in writing about anything missing.

Alaska-specific factors that affect claims

  • Short repair season. Exterior repairs, roofing and paving often have to wait for summer. Ask how the insurer will handle temporary repairs and delayed work, and keep records of weather delays.
  • Remote locations. In rural and off-road communities, materials and contractors may have to be shipped or flown in. Document those added costs so they can be included in estimates.
  • Freeze damage. Losing heat in winter can quickly lead to broken pipes. See ice dam and frozen pipe claims.
  • Earthquakes. Standard policies exclude earthquake damage. See earthquake insurance claims.

Rules insurers must follow

Alaska law prohibits unfair claim settlement practices (Alaska Statutes 21.36.125), and the state's regulations (3 AAC 26) set standards for how insurers acknowledge, investigate and respond to claims, including timelines. If you think your claim is being mishandled, the claim disputes guide explains your options, including contacting the Alaska Division of Insurance.

Frequently asked questions

How long do I have to file a homeowners claim in Alaska?

Your policy requires prompt notice of a loss and may set other deadlines, including for proof of loss and for filing suit. Report losses as soon as possible and read the conditions section of your policy.

Should I make repairs before the adjuster comes?

Make temporary repairs needed to prevent further damage and keep receipts, but document the damage thoroughly first and avoid permanent repairs until the insurer has inspected or agreed.

What is recoverable depreciation?

On many replacement cost policies, the insurer first pays actual cash value and withholds depreciation. You can recover the withheld amount after completing repairs, within the policy's deadlines.
