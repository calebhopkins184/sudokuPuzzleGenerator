Ice Dam and Frozen Pipe Insurance Claims: Winter Water Damage

Last updated November 2, 2026

Winter water damage is one of the most common homeowners claims in cold climates. Whether it's covered usually depends on two questions: was the water damage sudden and accidental, and did you take reasonable steps to prevent it, such as keeping the heat on? Reading those conditions in your policy before winter can prevent an unpleasant surprise.

Does homeowners insurance cover frozen pipes?

Many homeowners policies cover sudden water damage from a pipe that freezes and bursts, but common policy language excludes freezing damage when the home is vacant or unoccupied unless you kept heat on or shut off the water supply and drained the system. Wording varies, so check the freezing provisions in your policy.

  • Usually covered: Sudden, accidental water damage to walls, floors and belongings when a heated, occupied home's pipe bursts.
  • Often excluded or limited: Freeze damage in a vacant or unheated property where the water wasn't shut off and drained; long-term leaks; the cost of repairing the pipe itself under some forms; mold beyond policy limits.
  • Vacant properties: Many policies restrict coverage after a home has been vacant for a set period. Cabins and seasonal homes need policies that fit how they're used.

Are ice dams covered?

Interior water damage from an ice dam, where meltwater backs up under roofing and leaks into the house, is often covered as sudden and accidental damage. Removing the ice dam itself, fixing the underlying insulation or ventilation problem, and damage from long-term seepage are often not covered.

Damage from the weight of ice and snow collapsing a roof is a separate peril that many policies list as covered. Gutters torn off by ice may be treated differently by different insurers.

What about snowmelt flooding?

Water that enters from outside at ground level, such as snowmelt runoff or rising water during breakup, is generally considered flood or surface water and excluded from standard homeowners policies. Flood insurance through the National Flood Insurance Program or a private flood policy is separate. Water that backs up through drains or sewers is also commonly excluded unless you have a water backup endorsement.

First hours after a burst pipe or ice dam leak

  1. Stop the water. Shut off the main water valve. Know where it is before winter.
  2. Cut power if needed. If water is near outlets, fixtures or the electrical panel, shut off power safely or call an electrician.
  3. Document. Photograph and video the source, standing water, ceilings, walls and damaged belongings before cleanup.
  4. Call your insurer. Report the claim and ask about approved mitigation companies, though you can usually choose your own.
  5. Start drying. Water mitigation (extraction, dehumidifiers, removing wet drywall and insulation) prevents mold. Reasonable mitigation costs are generally part of the claim.
  6. Keep damaged materials or samples until the adjuster has seen them, or photograph them thoroughly with a measuring tape for scale.

Prevention that also protects your claim

  • Keep the thermostat at a safe temperature even when away (many sources suggest no lower than about 55°F).
  • Insulate pipes in exterior walls, crawlspaces and garages; use heat tape rated for the application.
  • Open cabinet doors under sinks on exterior walls during extreme cold.
  • For trips or seasonal homes, shut off the main and drain the system, or arrange for someone to check the house regularly and keep a record.
  • Consider a low-temperature alarm or smart water shutoff valve.
  • Reduce ice dams by air sealing the ceiling, adding attic insulation and keeping attic ventilation clear; use a roof rake from the ground to clear eaves safely.

These steps reduce the chance of a loss and also show you met policy conditions if a claim does happen.

Frequently asked questions

Will insurance cover frozen pipes if I was away?

It depends on the policy. Many cover freeze damage only if you maintained heat or shut off the water and drained the system, and vacancy provisions can limit coverage further.

Does homeowners insurance pay to remove an ice dam?

Often not. Policies commonly cover resulting interior water damage but not the cost of removing ice or correcting the insulation or ventilation problem.

Is basement flooding from snowmelt covered?

Usually not under a standard homeowners policy, which excludes flood and surface water. Flood insurance is a separate policy.
