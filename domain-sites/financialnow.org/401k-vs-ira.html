401(k) vs IRA: the differences and which to fund first

Updated November 2, 2026 · By the FinancialNow team

A 401(k) is a retirement plan offered through your employer, often with matching contributions and higher contribution limits. An IRA is an individual retirement account you open yourself, usually with more investment choices. Many people use both: 401(k) up to the match, then an IRA, then more 401(k).

The short comparison

Feature401(k)IRA
Who opens itYour employer sponsors the planYou open it at a bank or brokerage
Employer matchOften availableNo
Contribution limitMuch higher annual limitLower annual limit
Investment choicesA menu chosen by the planNearly any stock, bond, ETF, or mutual fund
ContributionsAutomatic payroll deductionsYou transfer money yourself (can be automated)
Roth optionIf the plan offers a Roth 401(k)Roth IRA, subject to income limits
Catch-up contributionsExtra allowed at age 50+Extra allowed at age 50+

Contribution limits change most years. Check the IRS pages on 401(k) contribution limits and IRA contribution limits for the current year's figures.

How a 401(k) works

You choose a percentage of each paycheck to contribute, and your employer sends it to the plan automatically. Key features:

  • Employer match. Many employers match part of what you contribute — for example, 50 cents per dollar up to 6% of pay. A match is an immediate return on your money, so contributing at least enough to get the full match is usually the first priority.
  • Vesting. Your own contributions are always yours. Employer contributions may vest over several years, meaning you keep them only after a set period of employment. Check your plan's summary plan description.
  • Limited menu. You pick from the funds your plan offers. Look for low-cost index funds or a target-date fund and check the expense ratios.
  • Loans. Some plans allow you to borrow from your balance. It can be useful in a pinch, but leaving a job can make the loan due quickly, and unpaid amounts may be taxed.

How an IRA works

An IRA is an account you open yourself at a brokerage, bank, or robo-advisor. You can contribute if you (or your spouse, if filing jointly) have earned income.

  • Wide investment choice. You can buy almost any index fund or ETF, often with no account fees.
  • Rollovers. When you leave a job, you can usually roll an old 401(k) into an IRA to keep it all in one place.
  • Deductibility limits. If you or your spouse are covered by a workplace plan, your ability to deduct traditional IRA contributions phases out at certain income levels.
  • Roth income limits. Direct Roth IRA contributions are reduced and then eliminated above certain income levels, which the IRS adjusts periodically.

Traditional vs Roth: when do you pay the tax?

Both 401(k)s and IRAs come in two tax flavors.

TraditionalRoth
ContributionsUsually pre-tax or tax-deductibleAfter-tax, no deduction now
GrowthTax-deferredTax-free
Qualified withdrawals in retirementTaxed as ordinary incomeTax-free
Required minimum distributionsYes, starting at the RMD age set by lawNone for Roth IRAs during the owner's lifetime; Roth 401(k)s no longer require them starting in 2024

A useful rule of thumb: Roth tends to win if you expect a higher tax rate in retirement than today (common early in a career), and traditional tends to win if you expect a lower rate later (common in peak earning years). Since nobody knows future tax law, many people split contributions between both for flexibility.

Withdrawal rules to know

  • Early withdrawals. Taking money out before age 59½ generally triggers income tax on pre-tax money plus a 10% additional tax, though there are exceptions. The IRS lists them in its exceptions to tax on early distributions page.
  • Roth IRA contributions (not earnings) can be withdrawn at any time without tax or penalty, because you already paid tax on them. Earnings generally require age 59½ and a five-year holding period to come out tax-free.
  • Required minimum distributions (RMDs). Traditional accounts require withdrawals starting at an age set by law. Under the SECURE 2.0 Act, that age is currently 73 for many people and is scheduled to rise to 75 for those born in 1960 or later. Confirm your own RMD age with the IRS.

A common order for funding retirement accounts

This is a widely used sequence, not personal advice:

  1. 401(k) up to the full employer match.
  2. Pay off high-interest debt and build an emergency fund.
  3. Fund an IRA (Roth or traditional) for more investment choice and potentially lower costs.
  4. Return to the 401(k) and raise contributions toward the annual maximum.
  5. If eligible, consider a Health Savings Account (HSA), which offers its own tax benefits for medical costs.
  6. Invest additional savings in a taxable brokerage account.

If your 401(k) has unusually high fees and no match, an IRA may deserve first place. If your income is modest, you may also qualify for the IRS Saver's Credit for retirement contributions.

Self-employed options

If you work for yourself, a Solo 401(k) or SEP IRA can allow larger contributions than a standard IRA. A SIMPLE IRA is common at small businesses. Each has its own rules and deadlines, so compare them with a tax professional.

Frequently asked questions

Can I have both a 401(k) and an IRA?

Yes. You can contribute to both in the same year, each up to its own limit. If you are covered by a workplace plan, your traditional IRA deduction may be limited at higher incomes, and Roth IRA eligibility depends on income.

What happens to my 401(k) when I leave my job?

You can usually leave it in the old plan if the balance meets the plan's minimum, roll it into your new employer's plan, roll it into an IRA, or cash it out. Cashing out typically triggers taxes and possibly a 10% penalty, so a direct rollover is usually the cleaner choice.
