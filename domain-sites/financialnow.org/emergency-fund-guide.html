Emergency fund guide: how much to save and where to keep it

Updated November 2, 2026 · By the FinancialNow team

An emergency fund is cash set aside only for unexpected, necessary expenses. Most people should aim for three to six months of essential expenses, kept in a safe, easy-to-reach account such as an FDIC-insured high-yield savings account.

Why an emergency fund comes first

An emergency fund keeps a bad week from turning into years of debt. Without one, a $1,200 car repair or a gap between jobs usually lands on a credit card, where interest rates commonly exceed 20%. With one, the same event is an inconvenience you pay for in cash and then rebuild.

It also protects your other goals. If you are investing for retirement, a cash cushion means you will not be forced to sell investments during a market downturn, or pull money from a 401(k) early and potentially owe taxes and a 10% penalty.

How much should you save?

The standard guideline is three to six months of essential expenses — not three to six months of income. Essential expenses are the bills you would still have to pay if you lost your job tomorrow.

Your situationSuggested target
Stable salary, two incomes, few dependentsAbout 3 months
Single income or dependents at home4–6 months
Freelance, commission, seasonal, or gig income6–12 months
Nearing retirement or in a volatile industry6+ months

Calculate your number

  1. Add up one month of essentials: housing, utilities, groceries, insurance premiums, transportation, phone, childcare, and minimum debt payments.
  2. Leave out things you would cut in a crisis, such as dining out, streaming services, and travel.
  3. Multiply by the number of months that fits your situation from the table.

For example, if your essentials are $3,000 a month and you have a steady job with a partner who also works, a three-month target is $9,000. If you are self-employed with the same costs, a six-month target is $18,000.

Start with a starter fund

Six months of expenses can feel impossible, so break it into stages:

  • Stage 1: $500 to $1,000. This covers the most common surprises, like a flat tire, a vet visit, or an urgent appliance repair.
  • Stage 2: one month of essentials. At this point most ordinary emergencies no longer require borrowing.
  • Stage 3: your full target. Build this after you have paid down high-interest debt, or in parallel if your income is unpredictable.

What counts as an emergency?

An emergency is unexpected, necessary, and urgent. A simple test: if it would not have been reasonable to plan for it, and waiting would make things worse, it probably qualifies.

  • Yes: job loss, a medical or dental bill, a car repair you need to get to work, an emergency flight for a family crisis, a broken furnace in winter.
  • No: holiday gifts, annual insurance premiums, vacations, a new phone because you want one.

Predictable but irregular costs, such as car registration or annual subscriptions, belong in separate sinking funds: small monthly savings earmarked for a known future bill. Keeping them separate stops your emergency fund from slowly draining.

Where to keep your emergency fund

The best place is somewhere safe, liquid, and separate from your everyday checking account.

OptionProsCons
High-yield savings accountInsured, earns interest, transfers in 1–2 business daysRates can change at any time
Money market deposit accountInsured, sometimes check-writingMay require higher minimums
Money market fund (brokerage)Often competitive yieldsNot FDIC-insured, though designed to be stable
Short-term CDs or Treasury billsLocked-in rateEarly withdrawal penalties or sale required
Stocks or stock fundsHigher long-term growthCan drop sharply right when you need cash

For most people, an online high-yield savings account at an FDIC-insured bank (or NCUA-insured credit union) is the simplest choice. FDIC insurance covers up to $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, for each account ownership category. You can confirm a bank's status with the FDIC's BankFind tool.

Keeping the money at a different bank from your checking account adds a little friction, which makes it harder to dip into for non-emergencies.

How to build it faster

  • Automate it. Schedule a transfer to savings on each payday so saving happens before spending.
  • Save windfalls. Put tax refunds, bonuses, and cash gifts toward the fund until it is full.
  • Trim one big bill. Shopping your car insurance or phone plan once can free up more than cutting dozens of small purchases.
  • Use a separate, named account. Labeling the account "Emergency fund" makes it psychologically harder to raid.
  • Track progress in your budget. Seeing the number climb is motivating.

After you use it

Using your emergency fund is not a failure — it is the point. Once the crisis is over, pause extra goals briefly and rebuild the fund with the same automatic transfers. Then reassess: if you needed more than you had, raise your target.

Frequently asked questions

Should my emergency fund be in a savings account or invested?

Keep it in cash or cash equivalents, such as an insured high-yield savings account. The purpose is stability and quick access, not growth. Investments can lose value right when emergencies tend to happen, such as during a recession.

Should I build an emergency fund or pay off debt first?

Many people build a starter fund of $500 to $1,000 first, then focus on high-interest debt, then finish a full three-to-six-month fund. That way a small surprise does not push you back onto the credit card while you pay it down.
