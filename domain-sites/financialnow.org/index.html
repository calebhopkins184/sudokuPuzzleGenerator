Personal finance basics, explained plainly

Personal finance comes down to a short list of habits: spend less than you earn, keep a cash cushion, avoid expensive debt, protect your credit, and invest the rest for the long term in low-cost, diversified funds. FinancialNow walks through each step in plain English, with worked examples and no product pitches.

The guides below are written in a sensible order. If you are just starting out, read them top to bottom. If you already have a budget and savings, jump straight to investing and retirement accounts.

A simple order of operations

Most people do best by tackling money goals in roughly this sequence. It is a rule of thumb, not a law, but it keeps you from investing while a 25% credit card balance quietly grows.

  1. Make a budget so you know where money goes each month. The 50/30/20 rule is an easy starting point.
  2. Build a starter emergency fund of roughly one month of expenses in a savings account.
  3. Capture any employer 401(k) match — it is part of your pay. See 401(k) vs IRA.
  4. Pay off high-interest debt using the avalanche or snowball method.
  5. Finish your full emergency fund of three to six months of essential expenses. See the emergency fund guide.
  6. Invest steadily in low-cost index funds through tax-advantaged accounts first.
  7. Protect your credit along the way so borrowing stays cheap when you need it. See how credit scores work.

Frequently asked questions

What is the first step in personal finance?

Track your spending and make a basic budget. You cannot plan savings, debt payoff, or investing until you know how much comes in and where it goes each month. A simple framework such as 50/30/20 is enough to start.

How much should I have in an emergency fund?

A common guideline is three to six months of essential expenses — rent or mortgage, utilities, groceries, insurance, transportation, and minimum debt payments. Aim for the higher end if your income is irregular or you are the only earner in your household.

Should I pay off debt or invest first?

Usually you should capture any employer retirement match first, then pay off high-interest debt such as credit cards, then invest more. Paying off a card charging over 20% interest is a guaranteed return that investing is unlikely to beat.

What is a good credit score?

On the 300–850 FICO scale, scores around 670–739 are generally considered good, 740–799 very good, and 800 or above exceptional. Lenders set their own cutoffs, but higher scores usually mean lower interest rates.

Are index funds good for beginners?

Index funds are a popular choice for beginners because they spread money across hundreds or thousands of companies at a low cost and require no stock picking. They still rise and fall with the market, so they suit money you will not need for at least five years.
