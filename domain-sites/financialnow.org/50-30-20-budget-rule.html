The 50/30/20 budget rule: a simple way to split your paycheck

Updated November 2, 2026 · By the FinancialNow team

The 50/30/20 rule is a simple budgeting framework: spend about 50% of your after-tax income on needs, 30% on wants, and put 20% toward savings and extra debt payments. It was popularized by Elizabeth Warren and Amelia Warren Tyagi in their 2005 book All Your Worth.

How the 50/30/20 rule works

The rule gives you three buckets instead of dozens of categories. You start with your after-tax income — your take-home pay, plus any pre-tax retirement contributions or health premiums deducted from your paycheck if you want a fuller picture — and split it like this:

BucketShareWhat goes in it
Needs50%Rent or mortgage, utilities, groceries, insurance, basic transportation, minimum debt payments, childcare
Wants30%Dining out, entertainment, travel, hobbies, upgrades beyond the basics, subscriptions
Savings and debt20%Emergency fund, retirement and investing, extra payments above the minimum on debt

The appeal is that you do not need to track every coffee. As long as each bucket stays near its target, the details are up to you.

Step-by-step: build your 50/30/20 budget

  1. Find your monthly after-tax income. Use your paystubs. If you are paid every two weeks, multiply one paycheck by 26 and divide by 12 — not by two, since some months have three paychecks.
  2. Calculate your targets. Multiply that income by 0.50, 0.30, and 0.20.
  3. List your needs. Pull the last two or three months of bank and card statements and total the essentials.
  4. Total your wants. Everything else you spent that was not savings or a required bill.
  5. Compare and adjust. If needs exceed 50%, look for the biggest fixed costs you can change. If savings are below 20%, automate a transfer on payday.

A worked example

Suppose your take-home pay is $4,200 per month.

BucketTargetExample spending
Needs (50%)$2,100Rent $1,350, utilities $150, groceries $380, car insurance and gas $220
Wants (30%)$1,260Restaurants $300, streaming $40, gym $45, shopping and fun $500, travel savings $375
Savings (20%)$840Emergency fund $300, Roth IRA $400, extra credit card payment $140

In this example, needs total exactly $2,100. If rent were $1,700 instead, needs would hit $2,450 — about 58% — and something else would have to give.

What counts as a need vs a want?

A need is something you must pay to live and work safely; a want is the upgrade. The line can be blurry, so be honest:

  • Groceries are a need; restaurant meals are a want.
  • A basic phone plan is a need; the newest phone on an installment plan is mostly a want.
  • Transportation to work is a need; the difference between a reliable used car and a luxury one is a want.
  • Minimum debt payments are a need; anything extra goes in the 20% bucket.

Adjusting the rule for real life

The percentages are a starting point, not a test you pass or fail.

When needs are more than 50%

In high-cost cities, rent alone can eat half of take-home pay. Options include a temporary 60/20/20 or 60/30/10 split while you work on the big levers: a roommate, a cheaper apartment at lease renewal, refinancing a loan, or raising income. Try to protect at least some savings, even 5% to 10%, so you are building the habit.

When you have high-interest debt

If you carry credit card balances, consider shrinking wants for a while — for example 50/20/30 — and sending the extra to debt using the avalanche or snowball method. The interest you avoid is a guaranteed return.

When you earn more

Higher earners can usually save well above 20%. A common approach is to keep lifestyle growth slower than income growth, sending raises mostly to savings.

Alternatives to 50/30/20

Tips to make it stick

  • Automate the 20% so it leaves your checking account on payday.
  • Review your three buckets once a month, not every day.
  • Keep a separate savings account for irregular bills like car repairs and gifts.
  • Recalculate whenever your income or rent changes.

When your budget has room in the savings bucket, put the first dollars toward an emergency fund, then toward long-term investing in low-cost index funds.

Frequently asked questions

Is the 50/30/20 rule based on gross or net income?

It is based on after-tax (net) income — what actually reaches your bank account. Some people add back pre-tax retirement contributions to their income and count them in the 20% savings bucket for a more complete picture.

Do minimum debt payments count as needs?

Yes. Minimum required payments are treated as needs because missing them causes real harm to your credit. Any payment above the minimum counts toward the 20% savings and debt bucket.
