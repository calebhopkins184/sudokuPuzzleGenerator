Index fund investing basics: a beginner's guide

Updated November 2, 2026 · By the FinancialNow team

An index fund is a mutual fund or ETF that holds all (or a representative sample) of the investments in a market index, such as the S&P 500. Instead of trying to beat the market, it aims to match it — at a very low cost — which is why index funds are a common foundation for long-term investors.

What is a market index?

A market index is a list of investments that represents a slice of the market, along with rules for how much of each to hold. Common examples:

IndexWhat it tracks
S&P 500About 500 large U.S. companies, weighted by market value
Total U.S. stock market (e.g., CRSP US Total Market)Nearly every publicly traded U.S. company, large and small
Total international stock marketCompanies in developed and emerging markets outside the U.S.
U.S. aggregate bond marketInvestment-grade U.S. government, corporate, and mortgage bonds

An index fund simply buys what the index holds. When the index changes, the fund changes. No manager is trying to pick winners.

The first index mutual fund for individual investors was launched in 1976 by Vanguard founder John Bogle. Today index funds are offered by nearly every major fund company and brokerage.

Why investors use index funds

  • Diversification. One fund can own hundreds or thousands of companies, so a single company's collapse has little effect.
  • Low cost. Because there is no stock-picking team, broad index funds often charge annual expense ratios well under 0.20%, and some charge a few hundredths of a percent.
  • Competitive results. Research such as S&P Dow Jones Indices' SPIVA reports has consistently found that a large majority of actively managed U.S. stock funds trail their benchmark index over long periods, largely because of higher fees.
  • Simplicity. You do not need to research individual companies or time the market.

Why costs matter so much

The expense ratio is the percentage of your balance a fund charges each year. Small differences compound into large ones. Here is what $10,000 grows to over 30 years, assuming a hypothetical 7% annual return before fees, with fees deducted each year:

Annual feeApproximate value after 30 years
0.00%$76,100
0.05%$75,000
0.20%$71,700
1.00%$56,300

The 1% fund costs roughly $19,000 more than the 0.05% fund on the same investment. Returns are hypothetical and not guaranteed, but the fee drag is real and predictable.

Index mutual funds vs index ETFs

You can own an index through either a mutual fund or an exchange-traded fund (ETF). Both can be excellent; the differences are mostly about mechanics.

FeatureIndex mutual fundIndex ETF
How it tradesOnce a day at the closing price (NAV)Throughout the day on an exchange, like a stock
Minimum investmentSometimes $1,000 to $3,000Price of one share, or less with fractional shares
Automatic investingEasy to schedule fixed-dollar purchasesDepends on your brokerage
Tax efficiency in taxable accountsGood for many index fundsOften slightly better due to how ETF shares are created and redeemed

How to start investing in index funds

  1. Have your basics covered. Build an emergency fund and pay off high-interest debt first. Invest money you will not need for at least five years.
  2. Pick the account. Tax-advantaged accounts usually come first: a workplace 401(k), especially to get any match, and an IRA. See 401(k) vs IRA. A regular taxable brokerage account works for additional savings.
  3. Choose your funds. A simple approach is one broad U.S. stock fund, one international stock fund, and one bond fund — often called a three-fund portfolio. A target-date index fund bundles all three and automatically becomes more conservative as its target year approaches.
  4. Decide your mix. More stocks means more growth potential and bigger swings. More bonds means a smoother ride with lower expected returns. Your time horizon and how you react to losses both matter.
  5. Automate contributions. Investing a fixed amount on a schedule, known as dollar-cost averaging, removes the temptation to time the market.
  6. Rebalance occasionally. Once a year, or when your mix drifts several percentage points from your target, sell a little of what grew and buy what lagged — ideally inside tax-advantaged accounts to avoid taxes.

What to look for when comparing index funds

  • The index itself. An S&P 500 fund and a total market fund are similar but not identical; a total market fund includes small and mid-size companies.
  • Expense ratio. Lower is better when two funds track the same index.
  • Tracking difference. How closely the fund's returns have matched its index after costs.
  • Account fees and minimums at the brokerage where you buy.

Risks to understand

Index funds are diversified, not safe from loss. A stock index fund will fall when the market falls, and broad markets have dropped by a third or more in severe downturns before eventually recovering. Bond funds can lose value when interest rates rise. Matching the market means you get the market's bad years too, which is why your timeline and your emergency fund matter.

Frequently asked questions

How much money do I need to start investing in index funds?

Very little. Many brokerages let you buy ETFs with fractional shares for a few dollars, and some index mutual funds have no minimum. Inside a 401(k), you can usually start with a small percentage of each paycheck.

Is an S&P 500 index fund enough diversification?

An S&P 500 fund owns about 500 large U.S. companies, which is broad, but it excludes smaller U.S. companies, international stocks, and bonds. Many investors add a total international fund and a bond fund, or use a target-date fund that includes all three.
