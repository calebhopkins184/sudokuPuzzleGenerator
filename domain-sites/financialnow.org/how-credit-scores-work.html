How credit scores work and how to raise yours

Updated November 2, 2026 · By the FinancialNow team

A credit score is a three-digit number, usually from 300 to 850, that estimates how likely you are to repay borrowed money. It is calculated from the information in your credit reports, and the biggest factors are paying on time and keeping credit card balances low.

Credit reports vs credit scores

Your credit report is the record; your credit score is a summary calculated from it.

  • Credit reports are kept by the three nationwide credit bureaus — Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion. They list your accounts, balances, payment history, public records such as bankruptcies, and inquiries from lenders.
  • Credit scores are calculated from those reports by scoring models. The most widely used are FICO scores and VantageScore, both of which typically range from 300 to 850.

Because each bureau may have slightly different information and lenders use different model versions, you have many scores, not one. They usually move together, so focus on the habits that drive all of them.

What goes into a FICO score

FICO publishes the general weight of each category for the general population:

FactorApproximate weightWhat it means
Payment history35%Whether you pay on time; late payments, collections, and bankruptcies hurt
Amounts owed30%Especially credit utilization: card balances relative to limits
Length of credit history15%Age of your oldest account and average age of accounts
New credit10%Recent applications and newly opened accounts
Credit mix10%Experience with both revolving credit (cards) and installment loans

Things that are not part of your score include your income, job, savings balance, age, race, religion, marital status, and where you live.

What the score ranges mean

FICO describes its ranges roughly as follows. Individual lenders set their own approval cutoffs.

FICO rangeRating
800–850Exceptional
740–799Very good
670–739Good
580–669Fair
300–579Poor

The practical difference shows up in interest rates. A borrower with a very good score will generally be offered a lower rate on a mortgage or car loan than one with a fair score, which can add up to thousands of dollars over the life of a loan.

How to check your credit for free

You are entitled to free credit reports from each of the three bureaus through AnnualCreditReport.com, the site authorized by federal law. The bureaus now offer free reports there weekly. Be wary of look-alike sites that require a credit card.

Free scores are widely available too: many credit card issuers, banks, and credit unions show a FICO or VantageScore in their apps. Checking your own credit is a soft inquiry and never lowers your score.

Review your report for errors

Look for accounts you do not recognize, late payments you believe are wrong, incorrect balances, and old negative items that should have aged off. If you find an error, you can dispute it directly with the bureau reporting it. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau has step-by-step dispute instructions and sample letters.

How to raise your credit score

1. Pay every bill on time

Payment history is the largest factor. Set up automatic minimum payments on every card and loan so you never miss one, then pay more manually. A payment is generally not reported late to the bureaus until it is at least 30 days past due, so if you miss a due date, pay immediately. Late payments can stay on your report for up to seven years, though their impact fades over time.

2. Lower your credit utilization

Utilization is your card balances divided by your credit limits. A common guideline is to stay below 30%, and people with the highest scores often keep it under 10%. Utilization is measured both per card and overall.

Example: if you have two cards with $5,000 limits each ($10,000 total) and carry $3,500 in balances, your utilization is 35%. Paying down to $900 brings it to 9%.

Because issuers usually report your statement balance, paying down a card before the statement closes can lower the utilization that gets reported. Unlike late payments, utilization has no memory: once balances drop, scores typically rebound quickly.

3. Keep old accounts open

Closing your oldest card can shorten your credit history and reduce your available credit, raising utilization. If a no-annual-fee card is not costing you anything, consider keeping it open and using it for a small recurring charge.

4. Apply for new credit sparingly

Each application for a loan or card usually triggers a hard inquiry. Hard inquiries stay on your report for two years, but FICO scores only consider those from the past 12 months, and the effect is usually small. When rate-shopping for a mortgage, auto loan, or student loan, FICO treats multiple inquiries within a short window as one, so shop within a couple of weeks.

5. Build credit if you are starting from scratch

  • A secured credit card, backed by a refundable deposit, reports to the bureaus like any card.
  • A credit-builder loan from a credit union holds your payments in savings and reports them as on-time.
  • Becoming an authorized user on a family member's well-managed card can add their account history to your report.

How long negative items last

ItemTypical time on report
Late paymentsUp to 7 years
Collections and charge-offsUp to 7 years
Chapter 7 bankruptcyUp to 10 years
Hard inquiries2 years (scored for about 12 months)

Positive accounts in good standing can remain much longer, which is another reason to keep old accounts open.

Frequently asked questions

Does checking my own credit score lower it?

No. Checking your own credit is a soft inquiry and does not affect your score. Only hard inquiries, which happen when you apply for new credit, can cause a small, temporary dip.

How fast can I raise my credit score?

Paying down credit card balances can raise a score within one or two billing cycles because utilization is recalculated each time balances are reported. Recovering from late payments or collections takes longer, often months to years of on-time payments.
