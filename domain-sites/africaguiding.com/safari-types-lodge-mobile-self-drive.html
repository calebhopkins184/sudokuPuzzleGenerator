Choosing a Safari Type: Lodge, Mobile, Self-Drive and More

The right safari type depends on three things: how much comfort you want, how much you are willing to plan and drive yourself, and how close to the wilderness you want to feel. A first-time visitor wanting ease usually does best with a guided lodge or tented-camp safari; experienced travelers on a budget often love self-driving in South Africa or Namibia.

Costs depend heavily on country, season, park fees, group size and how remote a camp is, so treat “relative cost” as a rough guide, not a quote.

Lodge safaris

A lodge safari means staying in a permanent building and going out on guided game drives, usually at dawn and late afternoon. Lodges are the easiest option: predictable comfort, good food, and often a pool for the hot midday hours. In private reserves such as those bordering South Africa’s Kruger National Park, lodge guides and trackers can often drive off-road to follow animals, which makes for very close sightings.

Be aware that large lodges inside busy national parks can feel less intimate, and in public parks you usually share sightings with other vehicles.

Tented camps

Tented camps put you in a large canvas tent, typically with a proper bed and private bathroom, and are the classic East and Southern African safari experience. Thin walls mean you hear lions roaring or hippos grazing at night, which many guests consider the highlight of the trip. Most camps are small, so service is personal. Staff escort guests after dark because wildlife walks through unfenced camps.

Mobile and seasonal camps

Mobile safaris move with the season or with you. Some operators run camps that relocate a few times a year to follow the Great Migration across the Serengeti; others set up private camps for a group and move every few days. Comfort ranges from simple dome tents to elegant setups with bucket showers. They suit travelers who prioritize location over amenities. Read more in our Great Migration guide.

Fly-in safaris

Fly-in safaris connect camps by light aircraft, saving long drives on rough roads. They are standard in Botswana’s Okavango Delta and common in Zambia and parts of Tanzania and Kenya. Two practical points:

  • Luggage limits are strict — often around 15–20 kg per person, in soft bags without wheels or frames. Confirm the exact rules with your operator.
  • Flights are weather- and weight-dependent, so schedules can shift.

Self-drive safaris

Self-drive means renting a vehicle and navigating parks yourself. It is the most independent and often the most affordable way to see wildlife, but you do all the spotting, planning and driving.

  • South Africa: the easiest self-drive country. Kruger has many tarred roads, clear rules, and a network of rest camps. A regular car works on many routes.
  • Namibia: superb but long distances on gravel roads. Many travelers rent a 4x4, often with rooftop tents. Drive slowly; gravel and fatigue cause most accidents.
  • Botswana: remote areas such as Moremi, Savuti and the Central Kalahari require a well-equipped 4x4, sand and recovery skills, and pre-booked campsites. Best for experienced overlanders.

Self-drive rules to respect: stay in your vehicle except at designated spots, keep to speed limits, never drive after the park’s gate closing times, and give elephants plenty of room.

Overland and group safaris

Overland trucks carry groups on long routes, such as Cape Town to Victoria Falls, camping most nights and sharing chores. They are budget-friendly and sociable, but game drives may be in the truck or optional extras, and days on the road can be long.

Walking and canoe safaris

Walking safaris swap the vehicle for an armed or highly trained guide and give you the small details: tracks, dung, birds, medicinal plants. Zambia’s South Luangwa, Zimbabwe’s Mana Pools and many Kenyan conservancies are known for them. Canoe safaris on the Zambezi and mokoro trips in the Okavango add a quiet, water-level view. Rules on minimum ages and fitness apply.

Questions to ask any operator

  1. How many guests per vehicle, and is a window seat guaranteed?
  2. Is the camp in a national park, a private reserve or a conservancy, and what activities are allowed there (night drives, walks, off-road driving)?
  3. What is included: park fees, drinks, transfers, laundry, tips?
  4. What are the luggage limits for any light-aircraft flights?
  5. What is the cancellation policy, and do they require travel insurance with medical evacuation?

Quick answers

What type of safari is best for a first trip?

A guided safari staying in lodges or tented camps is usually best for a first trip. Experienced guides find and interpret wildlife, logistics are handled, and you can focus on the experience.

Can you do a safari without a guide?

Yes. South Africa and Namibia are popular self-drive destinations, and Kruger National Park in particular has good roads and clear rules. Remote parts of Botswana are only suitable for experienced 4x4 drivers.
