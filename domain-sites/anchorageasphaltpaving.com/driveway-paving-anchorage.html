Driveway Paving in Anchorage: Building Asphalt That Survives Alaska Winters

Last updated November 2, 2026

A durable asphalt driveway in Anchorage is mostly about what's underneath it. The asphalt layer is only a few inches thick; the gravel base, drainage and soil below decide whether the surface stays flat through decades of freeze-thaw or starts heaving and cracking within a few winters.

What makes Anchorage driveways different?

Anchorage driveways have to survive long freezing seasons, repeated freeze-thaw cycles in spring and fall, frost-susceptible silty soils in many neighborhoods, heavy snow loads and plowing, and studded tires. Each of these punishes a thin base or poor drainage.

  • Frost action: When water in fine-grained soil freezes, it can form ice lenses that push the surface upward (frost heave). When they thaw in spring, the soil is saturated and weak, so the pavement can sag or crack under load.
  • Freeze-thaw cycling: Shoulder seasons in Southcentral Alaska often swing above and below freezing day to night. Water that gets into cracks freezes, expands and widens them.
  • Snow management: Plow blades, snowblower skids and piles of snow that melt and refreeze along edges all stress pavement, especially at unsupported edges.
  • Short construction window: Hot-mix asphalt has to be placed and compacted while it's hot, and cold air and ground cool it quickly. That compresses most residential paving into late spring through early fall (see our Alaska paving season guide).

How is a new asphalt driveway built?

A properly built driveway follows the same sequence no matter who does it: excavate unsuitable soil, place and compact a free-draining gravel base, grade it for drainage, then lay and compact hot-mix asphalt.

  1. Excavation. Topsoil, organic material and wet silt don't belong under pavement. In frost-susceptible ground, the contractor removes enough of it to make room for a thick gravel section. The right depth depends on your soil, and a reputable contractor will look at it before quoting.
  2. Base and subbase. Clean, well-graded gravel with few fines is placed in lifts and compacted. Gravel with little silt or clay drains quickly and is far less prone to frost heave than native silty soil. This layer is the single biggest factor in a driveway's lifespan in Alaska.
  3. Fine grading. The surface is shaped so water runs off, typically toward the street or a ditch and away from the house, garage slab and any spot where meltwater would pond and refreeze.
  4. Paving. Hot-mix asphalt is spread with a paver (or by hand on small jobs) and rolled while still hot. Residential driveways are commonly paved in a single lift a few inches thick after compaction; heavier use calls for more.
  5. Edges and transitions. Edges are compacted and ideally tapered or backed with gravel so they aren't left as a brittle, unsupported lip. The joint with the street and garage slab should be smooth so plows don't catch it.

Base depth and drainage: the parts you can't see

Ask every contractor how deep they plan to excavate, what material they'll use for the base, and how they'll compact it. Vague answers are a warning sign. Good practice includes:

  • Removing organics and soft spots entirely rather than paving over them.
  • Using a gravel base that is classified as non-frost-susceptible or close to it, placed in lifts and compacted with a plate compactor or roller.
  • Sloping the surface so there are no low spots. Even a shallow birdbath becomes an ice patch and a source of water infiltration.
  • Keeping roof downspouts and snow-pile meltwater from draining across or under the pavement.

If you already have a gravel driveway that has been stable for years, it may serve as a starting base, but it usually still needs regrading, added material and compaction. A driveway that has a history of heaving or soft spots needs those areas dug out, not just covered.

Permits and the street connection

Work in the public right-of-way, such as a new or widened driveway apron where it meets a city street, generally needs approval from the Municipality of Anchorage. Requirements change, so check with the Municipality (or your road service area or HOA outside the city's core) before work begins, and ask whether your contractor will pull any required permit. Call 811 before any digging so buried utilities are located.

Caring for a new driveway

  • First weeks: Fresh asphalt stays soft, especially on warm, sunny days. Avoid parking in the same spot every day, turning steering wheels while stopped, kickstands, trailer jacks and sharp-edged objects.
  • First winter: Use a plow or snowblower set slightly above the surface, or with poly or rubber cutting edges, and avoid piling snow on the edges.
  • Ongoing: Fill cracks early (see crack filling and frost heave repair) and consider sealcoating once the asphalt has cured.

Frequently asked questions

How long does an asphalt driveway last in Anchorage?

Lifespan depends far more on base construction, drainage and maintenance than on the asphalt itself. A driveway built on a thick, well-drained gravel base and kept crack-sealed can last for decades; one paved over frost-susceptible silt may crack and heave within a few winters.

Can I pave over my existing gravel driveway?

Often yes, if the gravel has been stable and drains well. It still needs regrading, added base material where thin, and thorough compaction. Areas that heave or go soft in spring should be excavated and rebuilt first.

When can I drive on a new asphalt driveway?

Contractors commonly advise waiting at least a few days before driving on new residential asphalt and longer in hot weather. Follow your installer's instructions, which account for the mix and conditions on your job.
