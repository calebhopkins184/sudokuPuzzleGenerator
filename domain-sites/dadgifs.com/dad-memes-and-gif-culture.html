Dad Memes and GIF Culture, Explained

A GIF is a short, looping image file format that became the internet’s favorite way to react without words. Dad memes and dad joke GIFs combine that format with the wholesome, groan-inducing humor of dad jokes. Here’s where both came from, why they work, and how to share them responsibly.

By the Dad GIFs editorial team · Updated November 2, 2026

A very short history of the GIF

The GIF (Graphics Interchange Format) was introduced by CompuServe in 1987, developed by a team led by engineer Steve Wilhite. It compressed images efficiently for slow connections, which made it popular on the early web.

  • Animation: the format could store multiple frames, and in the mid-1990s Netscape Navigator added support for looping them, which made the endlessly repeating animated GIF possible.
  • Limitations: each frame can use at most 256 colors, which is why GIFs often look grainy or banded.
  • Revival: in the 2010s, social media and messaging apps turned GIFs into a language of reactions. Oxford American Dictionaries even named “GIF” (as a verb) its word of the year for 2012.

Today, many platforms that show “GIFs” actually serve video formats such as MP4 or WebP behind the scenes, because they look better and load faster at a fraction of the file size.

Jif or gif?

Steve Wilhite said it’s pronounced “jif,” like the peanut butter brand. Plenty of people say it with a hard g, as in “gift,” and major dictionaries list both pronunciations. Arguing about it at the dinner table is, naturally, extremely dad behavior.

Where the “dad joke” came from

Corny puns are as old as language, but the phrase “dad joke” became widely used in the 2000s and 2010s, and it now has entries in major dictionaries such as Merriam-Webster. Online communities devoted to dad jokes have attracted large followings, and the genre crossed over into the mainstream through social media, late-night TV and countless family group chats.

What makes a dad joke different from other jokes is the relationship between teller and audience. The joke is meant to be a little bad, and everyone knows it. The teller’s pride and the audience’s groan are the real performance.

How dad memes work

A meme is an idea, image or format that spreads and gets remixed. Common dad-meme formats include:

  • The setup-punchline image: a dad joke written over a photo, with the punchline at the bottom.
  • The reaction GIF: a looping clip of someone groaning, facepalming, slow-clapping or proudly pointing finger guns — used to respond to a pun.
  • The relatable dad: captions about universal dad habits — adjusting the thermostat, arriving at the airport four hours early, or announcing the car’s fuel mileage.
  • The text-message exchange: screenshots of a dad replying to a serious message with a pun.
  • Wholesome dad content: videos and posts of fathers being kind, supportive or teaching useful skills, which became a beloved corner of the internet.

Sharing GIFs legally

Most popular reaction GIFs are clips of movies, TV shows or other copyrighted material. Sharing them through the built-in GIF search in apps such as messaging platforms is generally how people use them, and those services set their own terms of use. But if you’re using GIFs on a website, in marketing or for anything commercial, the rules are stricter.

  • Don’t assume a GIF is free to use just because it’s online. Copyright usually belongs to the original creator or studio.
  • Fair use (in the US) and similar exceptions elsewhere depend on specific facts; they are not a blanket permission for commercial use.
  • Make your own. The safest GIF is one you created from your own video or illustrations. Many phones can turn a short video or burst of photos into a GIF.
  • Use licensed sources and follow their terms, crediting creators where required.

That’s why this site uses simple drawings and CSS animation instead of borrowed clips. (This isn’t legal advice; for business use, check with a qualified professional.)

Sharing GIFs kindly and accessibly

  • Add alt text. Describe the GIF briefly so people using screen readers get the joke too: “Man slowly lowering sunglasses in disbelief.”
  • Avoid flashing content. Rapid flashing can trigger seizures in people with photosensitive epilepsy. Accessibility guidelines advise against content that flashes more than three times per second.
  • Respect reduced-motion settings. On websites, honor the user’s “reduce motion” preference and provide a way to pause animation. This site’s little animations stop automatically when that setting is on.
  • Read the room. A groaning GIF is perfect for a pun, less so for a serious conversation.

Make your own dad joke GIF

  1. Write a short, punchy joke — see our pun guide.
  2. Record a three-to-five-second video: the setup, a dramatic pause, the punchline and a proud grin.
  3. Convert it using your phone’s built-in tools or a reputable app.
  4. Add captions for people watching without sound.
  5. Share it with the family and wait for the groans.
