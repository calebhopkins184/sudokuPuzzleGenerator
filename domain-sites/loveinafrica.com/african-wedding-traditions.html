African Wedding Traditions: A Respectful Introduction

There is no single “African wedding.” Africa has 54 countries and thousands of ethnic groups, each with its own customs. Still, many traditions share a core idea: marriage joins two families, not just two people, so families play a central role in negotiations, gifts and ceremonies. Here are some well-known traditions, described with the caveat that practices vary between families, regions and generations.

By the Love in Africa editorial team · Updated November 2, 2026

Southern Africa: lobola

Lobola is the practice, common among many Southern African peoples, in which the groom’s family offers a bride price to the bride’s family. It is known by different names, including lobola or lobolo in Zulu and Xhosa, bohali in Sesotho and roora in Shona. Traditionally it was paid in cattle; today it is often settled in money, sometimes still expressed as a number of cattle.

Negotiations are carried out by family representatives, frequently uncles, rather than the couple. The process can be formal, ritualized and even good-humored, and it is understood as a way of building respect and a bond between families. Attitudes to lobola vary: many couples value it as cultural heritage, while others debate how it should be adapted.

Zulu weddings may include further stages, such as umembeso, when the groom’s family presents gifts to the bride’s family, and umabo, when the bride’s side presents gifts, such as blankets, to the groom’s family.

Nigeria: Yoruba and Igbo ceremonies

Many Nigerian couples hold a traditional ceremony in addition to a church, mosque or civil wedding.

  • Yoruba traditional engagement: a lively event guided by two masters of ceremonies, one representing each family. The groom and his friends traditionally prostrate before the bride’s family as a sign of respect, and families exchange items listed in advance. The bride often makes a later entrance and chooses her groom from the group.
  • Igbo igba nkwu (wine carrying): the bride searches through the crowd holding a cup of palm wine. When she finds the groom, she kneels and offers it to him, and his drinking from it signals her choice.
  • Aso ebi: family and friends wear coordinated fabric or colors chosen for the event, creating a striking visual unity.
  • Money spraying: guests shower dancing couples with banknotes as a joyful blessing.

Ghana: knocking and traditional marriage

In many Ghanaian communities, especially among the Akan, marriage begins with the “knocking” ceremony (often called kokooko), when the groom’s family formally visits the bride’s family to announce their intentions. A traditional marriage ceremony follows, where an agreed list of items is presented and accepted by the bride’s family. Kente cloth, with its colorful woven patterns, is a hallmark of many Ghanaian celebrations. Libation, pouring a drink to honor ancestors, may open proceedings.

North Africa: Moroccan weddings

Traditional Moroccan weddings are multi-day affairs. Common elements include:

  • Hammam ritual: the bride visits the bathhouse with female relatives and friends before the wedding.
  • Henna night: intricate henna designs are applied to the bride’s hands and feet, accompanied by music and celebration.
  • The negafa: an attendant who dresses the bride and manages her several outfit changes, from caftans to regional styles.
  • The amariya: the bride, and sometimes the groom, is lifted and carried on an ornate platform as guests celebrate.

East Africa: Swahili coast traditions

On the Swahili coast of Kenya and Tanzania, Muslim weddings often feature henna (hina) for the bride, women-only celebrations with music and dance, and a ceremony in which the marriage contract is concluded. Taarab music, which blends African, Arabic and Indian influences, is a traditional accompaniment in places such as Zanzibar and Mombasa.

The diaspora: jumping the broom

Jumping the broom is a tradition strongly associated with African American weddings. It became significant during slavery in the United States, when enslaved people were denied legal marriage, and it was revived as a celebration of heritage in the late twentieth century. Its deeper origins are debated by historians, with suggested roots in both West Africa and the British Isles. Today, many couples see it as a powerful symbol of sweeping away the past and starting a household together.

How to honor traditions respectfully

If you are incorporating traditions from your own heritage, your family and elders are the best guides. If a tradition comes from a culture that isn’t yours — for example, at a destination wedding — keep these points in mind:

  1. Ask, don’t assume. Speak to people from the culture, and a local planner, about what is appropriate for outsiders.
  2. Avoid sacred or status-specific items. Some garments, objects and rituals carry religious or social meaning that isn’t meant for casual use.
  3. Involve practitioners. Hire local musicians, dancers, henna artists or MCs, so traditions are performed authentically and people are paid for their skills.
  4. Explain to guests. A short note in the program helps everyone appreciate what they’re seeing.
  5. Remember diversity. Avoid calling anything “the African way.” Name the specific culture.

Quick answers

What is lobola?

Lobola is a Southern African tradition in which the groom’s family offers a bride price, historically cattle and now often money, to the bride’s family after negotiations between family representatives. It symbolizes respect and the joining of two families.

Is jumping the broom an African tradition?

It is most strongly associated with African American history and weddings. Its earlier origins are debated, with possible roots in West Africa and the British Isles.
