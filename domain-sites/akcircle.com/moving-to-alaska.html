Moving to Alaska: A Practical Relocation Guide

Updated November 2, 2026 · By the AK Circle team

Moving to Alaska takes more planning than a typical interstate move because most of the state is far from the Lower 48 and much of it isn't on a road system at all. The essentials are: choose your community first (it determines everything else), decide how to move your vehicle and belongings (drive, ship or ferry), budget for higher costs on food and goods, and complete residency tasks such as your Alaska driver's license and vehicle registration soon after you arrive.

Step 1: Pick a community, not just "Alaska"

Alaska is enormous, and daily life varies more between regions than between many states. Roughly half of Alaskans live in the Anchorage and Matanuska-Susitna (Mat-Su) area.

How major Alaska regions differ for newcomers
AreaRoad accessWhat to expect
AnchorageYesThe largest city, with the most jobs, healthcare and shopping. Milder winters than the Interior; mountains and trails at the edge of town.
Mat-Su (Wasilla, Palmer)YesFast-growing area with more land and generally lower housing costs than Anchorage. Many residents commute to Anchorage, so expect winter driving.
Fairbanks / InteriorYesVery cold, dry winters (−40° happens) and warm summers. Military bases and the University of Alaska Fairbanks are big employers. Excellent aurora viewing.
Kenai PeninsulaYesFishing, tourism and oil-related work in towns like Soldotna, Kenai, Homer and Seward. More of a small-town feel.
Juneau / SoutheastNo (ferry or air)Rainforest climate: mild, wet and dark in winter. Juneau is the state capital, but you can't drive there from the rest of North America.
Rural & Bush communitiesMostly noAccess by small plane or seasonal barge. Very high costs for fuel and groceries. Housing is often tight. Talk to locals or your employer before committing.

Step 2: Choose how to get there

There are three main ways to move to Alaska: drive the Alaska Highway through Canada, ship your vehicle and goods by sea from the Pacific Northwest, or ride the Alaska Marine Highway ferry. Many people combine them, for example flying north while a vehicle and household goods travel by ship.

Driving the Alaska Highway (ALCAN)

The Alaska Highway runs from Dawson Creek, British Columbia, to Delta Junction, Alaska. From the Seattle area to Anchorage is roughly 2,300 miles of driving, typically spread over five to seven days.

  • Border paperwork: You cross into Canada and back into the U.S., so every traveler needs acceptable travel documents (such as a passport). Check Canada Border Services Agency rules on firearms, pets, alcohol, cannabis and food before you go; firearms rules in particular are strict.
  • Services are spread out: Fill up whenever you can in northern B.C. and the Yukon, carry a full-size spare, and expect long stretches without cell service.
  • Season matters: Late spring through early fall is easiest. Winter drives are possible but require winter tires, survival gear and flexibility for road closures.
  • Wildlife on the road: Bison, moose, caribou and bears are common roadside sights. Slow down, especially at dawn and dusk.

Shipping a vehicle and household goods

Ocean carriers move freight and vehicles between the Tacoma/Seattle area and Anchorage on a regular schedule, and other services reach Southeast and Kodiak. Moving companies can handle door-to-door shipping, or you can use a shipping container or freight-only service and load it yourself. Get several written quotes, ask what is and isn't insured, and plan on your belongings arriving days to weeks after you do.

Alaska Marine Highway ferry

The state ferry system connects Bellingham, Washington, with Southeast Alaska communities and onward ports. It's the only way to bring a vehicle to Juneau without a barge. Sailings book up in summer, and schedules change by season, so check the Alaska Marine Highway System's current schedule early.

Step 3: Budget for Alaska costs

Expect to pay more for most goods than in the Lower 48, especially outside Anchorage, Fairbanks and Juneau. Groceries, building materials, vehicle parts and anything heavy cost more because of shipping. On the other side of the ledger, Alaska has no state income tax and no statewide sales tax, although many cities and boroughs charge local sales taxes.

  • Housing: Inventory can be tight, especially in summer when seasonal workers and military moves peak. Rent short-term first if you can, so you can learn neighborhoods before buying.
  • Heating: Ask landlords or sellers for past winter heating bills. Fuel type (natural gas, heating oil, propane, wood, electric) makes a big difference.
  • Vehicle: All-wheel or four-wheel drive is helpful but not required in town; good winter tires matter more.
  • Travel home: Budget for flights Outside to see family. Many Alaskans treat this as a fixed annual expense.

Step 4: Become an Alaska resident

Once you've moved, take care of residency tasks promptly. Requirements and deadlines change, so confirm details with the Alaska Division of Motor Vehicles and other agencies listed below.

  1. Driver's license: New residents need to get an Alaska license after establishing residency; the DMV sets the deadline and required documents.
  2. Vehicle registration and title: Register your vehicle with the Alaska DMV. Some areas, such as the Municipality of Anchorage, add local vehicle taxes or fees at registration.
  3. Voter registration: You can register with the Alaska Division of Elections.
  4. Permanent Fund Dividend (PFD): You generally must be an Alaska resident for an entire calendar year, intending to stay indefinitely, before you can apply the following year. Someone who moves in mid-year typically waits more than a year for a first dividend. See the PFD Division for the rules on eligibility and allowable absences.
  5. Resident hunting and fishing licenses: Resident license rates generally require 12 consecutive months of residency. Until then you buy nonresident licenses from the Alaska Department of Fish and Game.

Step 5: Prepare for your first winter

Your first winter is the biggest adjustment. Arrive with, or buy soon after arriving, a real winter coat, insulated boots, traction cleats and winter tires. Learn about engine block heaters and plug-ins, and plan ways to stay active and social through the dark months. Our winter living guide covers this in detail, and the daylight and seasons guide explains what the light is really like.

Jobs and the Alaska economy

Major employers include government (federal, state, local and tribal), healthcare, the military, oil and gas, fishing and seafood processing, tourism, transportation, and construction. Much of the work is seasonal, peaking in summer. If your job is seasonal, plan to stretch summer income across the whole year. Look for jobs through the Alaska Department of Labor and Workforce Development, employer sites and local networks; many Alaska positions are filled through word of mouth.

Official resources to bookmark: Alaska DMV (licenses and registration), Alaska PFD Division (dividend eligibility), Alaska Department of Fish and Game (licenses), Alaska Marine Highway System (ferry schedules), Alaska Department of Labor and Workforce Development (jobs).
