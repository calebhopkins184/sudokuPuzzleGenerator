Northern Lights in Alaska: When and Where to See the Aurora

Updated November 2, 2026 · By the AK Circle team

The best time to see the northern lights in Alaska is from late August to mid-April, on clear, dark nights, usually between about 10 p.m. and 2 a.m. The best places are in and north of Fairbanks, which sit under the "auroral oval," the ring-shaped zone where aurora is most frequent. You can also see it from Anchorage and Southcentral Alaska on stronger nights if you get away from city lights.

What causes the aurora?

The aurora borealis happens when charged particles from the Sun, carried by the solar wind, are funneled by Earth's magnetic field toward the polar regions. They collide with oxygen and nitrogen in the upper atmosphere, which then give off light. Oxygen produces the common green color (and rarer red at higher altitudes), while nitrogen can add purple, pink and blue edges. Strong solar activity, such as coronal mass ejections, pushes aurora farther south and makes displays brighter.

When is aurora season in Alaska?

Aurora season is roughly late August through mid-April. The aurora is active year-round, but in summer the sky in most of Alaska never gets dark enough to see it. Within the season:

  • Late August to September: Darkness returns, temperatures are mild, and lakes can reflect the lights. Weather can be cloudy.
  • October to November: Longer nights, but this is often a cloudier time of year in many areas.
  • December to February: The longest nights and, in the Interior, often clear, very cold skies. Prepare for serious cold.
  • March: A favorite for many visitors: nights are still dark, days are longer and more comfortable, and clear weather is common in the Interior.

Around the spring and fall equinoxes, geomagnetic activity tends to be a bit higher on average, a pattern scientists have documented for decades. It doesn't guarantee anything on a given night, but it's one reason September and March are popular.

What time of night is best?

Activity often peaks around local magnetic midnight, which in Alaska falls roughly between 11 p.m. and 2 a.m. Displays can happen anytime it's dark, though, including early evening and just before dawn. Patience pays: aurora often pulses in waves, with quiet periods of 20–60 minutes between bursts.

How to read an aurora forecast

You need three things for a good show: geomagnetic activity, clear skies and darkness. Check all three.

Where to see the northern lights in Alaska

Fairbanks and the Interior

Fairbanks is the classic aurora base: it's under the auroral oval, has reliable winter darkness and is often clear. Get a few miles out of town to escape city glow. Popular areas include the hills north of town and roads toward Chena Hot Springs. Lodges and tour operators run aurora-viewing trips with warm cabins.

Farther north: Coldfoot and Wiseman

Along the Dalton Highway, Coldfoot and Wiseman are even closer to the heart of the auroral zone and have almost no light pollution. The Dalton is a remote industrial road with long gaps between services, so most visitors go with a tour or fly in.

Anchorage and Southcentral

Anchorage sees fewer displays and has more light pollution and clouds, but strong nights can be spectacular. Good dark spots within reach include the Glen Alps area in Chugach State Park, the Knik River area, Hatcher Pass near Palmer, and pullouts along Turnagain Arm. Look north, away from the city glow, and check road conditions before heading up mountain roads in winter.

Denali area

The area around Denali National Park is dark and scenic, but many services there are seasonal and shut down in winter. Plan ahead in late August and September when lodges are still open and darkness has returned.

How to photograph the aurora

A tripod and manual settings matter more than an expensive camera. Here's a starting point you can adjust:

  • Focus: Manual focus at infinity. Test on a bright star using live view, then tape the focus ring.
  • Aperture: As wide as your lens allows (f/1.8–f/2.8 is ideal).
  • ISO: 1600–3200 to start, lower if the aurora is bright.
  • Shutter: 2–15 seconds. Use shorter exposures for fast-moving, bright aurora so detail doesn't blur.
  • Phones: Most recent phones have a night mode that works well on a tripod or braced against something solid.
  • Batteries: Cold drains them fast. Keep spares in an inside pocket and swap often.
  • Condensation: When you come back inside, put the camera in a sealed bag first so moisture condenses on the bag, not inside the lens.

Staying safe and comfortable

Aurora watching means standing still in the cold at night. Dress in layers with insulated boots and mitts, bring a hot drink, and keep your vehicle fueled. Pull fully off the road, never stop in a traffic lane, and avoid frozen lakes unless you know the ice is safe. Let someone know where you're going if you head out of town.

Frequently asked questions

Can you see the northern lights in Alaska in summer?

Usually not. The aurora still occurs, but from roughly late April to mid-August the sky in most of Alaska stays too bright at night to see it.

Is Fairbanks or Anchorage better for the northern lights?

Fairbanks is generally better. It sits under the auroral oval, has clearer winter skies and sees aurora on far more nights. Anchorage can have great displays during stronger geomagnetic activity.

What Kp do I need to see the aurora in Anchorage?

There's no fixed cutoff, but Anchorage usually needs moderate activity, often around Kp 4–5 or higher, to see lights well above the horizon. Clear, dark skies away from city lights matter just as much.
