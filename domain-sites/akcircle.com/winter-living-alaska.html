Alaska Winter Living Tips: Cars, Homes, Clothing and Mindset

Updated November 2, 2026 · By the AK Circle team

Living well through an Alaska winter comes down to four things: a vehicle prepared for cold starts and icy roads, a home protected against frozen pipes and ice dams, clothing built on layers, and habits that keep you active and connected through the dark months. Here's what experienced Alaskans do.

Your vehicle in winter

Engine block heaters and plugging in

An engine block heater warms your engine's coolant so it starts easily and wears less in the cold. Many Alaska vehicles have one, with a plug poking out of the grille. A common rule of thumb is to plug in when temperatures drop to around 20°F or below. Most people use a timer to run the heater for a few hours before they leave rather than all night, which saves electricity. In very cold areas like Fairbanks, people also use battery blankets, oil pan heaters and sometimes transmission heaters. Many workplaces and apartments in the Interior provide outlets in parking areas.

Tires

Good winter tires matter more than four-wheel drive. Options include studless winter tires (soft rubber compounds made for ice and snow) and studded tires. Alaska allows studded tires only during a seasonal window that varies by latitude, and some areas charge a tire stud fee. Check Alaska DMV for current dates. Many Alaskans switch tires in early fall to beat the rush at shops.

Winter car kit

  • Snow brush, ice scraper and a small shovel
  • Sleeping bag or warm blankets, extra hat, mitts and boots
  • Jumper cables or a portable jump starter
  • Traction aid (sand, kitty litter or traction mats)
  • Flashlight or headlamp with spare batteries
  • Water, snacks and a phone charger
  • Tow strap and a reflective triangle or flares

Keep your gas tank at least half full. It reduces condensation in the tank and gives you a margin if you're stuck. Never let a vehicle idle with the tailpipe blocked by snow, which can push carbon monoxide into the cabin.

Driving tips

  • Leave much more following distance; stopping on ice can take several times longer than on dry pavement.
  • Watch for black ice on bridges and shaded curves, and for ice fog in cold Interior towns.
  • Use headlights all day; winter light is low.
  • Be alert for moose, especially at dusk and dark, as they're hard to see and often cross roads in winter.
  • Check road conditions through the state's 511 traveler information before long drives.

Your home in winter

Prevent frozen pipes

  • Know where your main water shutoff is before you need it.
  • Insulate pipes in crawl spaces, garages and exterior walls; use heat tape rated for the job where needed and inspect it every fall.
  • Keep heat on when you travel, and have someone check the house regularly. A furnace failure during a cold snap can freeze a home within hours.
  • Open cabinet doors under sinks on exterior walls during extreme cold.
  • Disconnect garden hoses and drain outdoor faucets before freeze-up.

Ice dams and roof snow

Ice dams form when heat escaping through the roof melts snow, which refreezes at the cold eaves. Water then backs up under shingles and can leak into walls and ceilings. The long-term fix is better attic insulation and ventilation so the roof stays cold. In the short term, a roof rake used from the ground can pull snow off the lower edge of the roof. Avoid climbing on icy roofs, and hire help if heavy snow loads become a concern.

Heating and safety

  • Have your furnace or boiler serviced every fall.
  • Install carbon monoxide alarms near sleeping areas and on each level, and test smoke alarms monthly.
  • If you burn wood, have the chimney inspected and cleaned regularly to prevent chimney fires, and follow any local burn restrictions during air-quality alerts (common in Fairbanks).
  • Keep exterior vents for furnaces, boilers and direct-vent heaters clear of snow.
  • Have a backup plan for outages: extra blankets, flashlights, a safe backup heat source if possible, and water stored in case pumps lose power.

Moisture and frost

Cold outside air holds little moisture, so indoor air gets very dry. Some people use humidifiers for comfort, but too much indoor humidity causes frost and condensation on windows and in walls. Aim for a moderate level and use bathroom and kitchen fans.

Dressing for Alaska cold

Layering lets you adjust as you move between cold outdoors and warm buildings:

  1. Base layer: Wool or synthetic that wicks sweat. Avoid cotton, which stays wet and cold.
  2. Insulating layer: Fleece, wool or a down or synthetic puffy.
  3. Shell: A wind- and water-resistant outer layer, especially in windy coastal towns.

Add insulated boots with room for thick socks, mittens (warmer than gloves), a hat that covers your ears, and a neck gaiter. Traction cleats that slip over your boots prevent many winter falls. Watch for frostbite on exposed skin in wind and extreme cold: numbness and white or grayish patches are warning signs.

Wildlife in winter

Moose move into neighborhoods in winter because deep snow makes travel hard and city plantings are easy food. A moose with ears back and hackles raised is agitated; back away and put something between you and it. Moose injure people more often than bears do in Alaska. Keep dogs leashed, and don't walk past a moose on a narrow trail. Bears mostly den in winter, but can be out in early spring or during warm spells.

Staying happy through the dark months

  • Embrace a winter activity. Cross-country skiing, fat biking, skating, snowshoeing or ice fishing. Alaskans who love winter almost always have a winter sport.
  • Get daylight. Get outside during midday light whenever possible, and consider a light therapy box after talking with a health provider.
  • Join something. Community classes, ski clubs, church or volunteer groups, and library events help newcomers build friendships.
  • Plan a mid-winter break. Many Alaskans schedule a trip somewhere sunny in January or February.
  • Celebrate the light. After the December solstice, daylight returns quickly. By March, days are long and bright, and the snow is often at its best.

Frequently asked questions

When should I plug in my car in Alaska?

A common rule of thumb is to plug in your engine block heater when temperatures are around 20°F or colder, using a timer to run it for a few hours before you drive. Check your vehicle manual for specifics.

Do I need studded tires in Anchorage?

Not necessarily. Quality studless winter tires work well for many drivers. Studded tires are allowed only during a seasonal window set by the state, so check Alaska DMV for current dates and any fees.
