How to use free trials without getting charged

Updated November 2, 2026 · By the 15DayTrial team

To use a free trial without getting charged, write down the exact date it converts to a paid plan, set a reminder at least a day or two before, and cancel through the same place you signed up if you do not want to pay. Most trials require a payment method precisely because they renew automatically unless you act.

Why free trials want your card

Most free trials are negative option offers: your silence counts as agreement to pay. The company collects a card or app-store payment method up front so that the trial converts automatically. That is legal when the terms are clearly disclosed and you agree to them, but it means the burden is on you to cancel on time.

Step 1: Read the five terms that matter

Before you start any trial, find these details — usually near the "Start trial" button, in the checkout screen, or in a linked terms page:

TermWhat to look for
Trial length and end time"15 days," "1 month," or a specific date; some convert at a particular hour
Price after the trialMonthly or annual, and whether taxes are added
Billing cycleAn annual plan can mean a large single charge
Cancellation methodApp-store settings, website, chat, or phone
What happens if you cancel earlyWhether you keep access until the trial ends

Take a screenshot of the offer and save the confirmation email. If there is ever a dispute, proof of the terms you agreed to is your strongest evidence.

Step 2: Count the days correctly

Trial lengths are easy to misjudge. A 15-day trial that starts on November 2 generally converts around November 17 — but the exact timing depends on the company. Some count from the minute you signed up; others bill on the date shown in your account.

The safest approach is to look up the renewal date in your account or subscription settings right after you sign up, and rely on that instead of your own math. In Apple and Google Play subscription settings, the renewal date is listed for each subscription.

Step 3: Set reminders you will not ignore

  • Add two calendar events: one three days before the end date, one the day before.
  • Put the cancellation link or path in the reminder itself, so canceling takes seconds.
  • Add the trial to your subscription tracker.

Apple, for example, says that to avoid renewing a trial subscription you should cancel at least 24 hours before the trial ends. Treat "the day before" as your real deadline for any trial.

Step 4: Consider canceling immediately

If you know you only want the free period, canceling right after signing up eliminates the risk of forgetting. Two caveats:

  • Some services end access right away when you cancel during a trial. Apple notes that if you cancel during a free trial, you might lose access immediately. Others keep access until the trial would have ended.
  • Some offers only apply once. If you cancel and later want the service, you may not get another trial.

If you are not sure which applies, search the service's help center for "cancel free trial," or simply rely on reminders instead.

Step 5: Use a payment method that helps you

  • App-store billing (Apple or Google Play) puts all subscriptions in one list with clear renewal dates and a cancel button, which makes trials easier to manage.
  • Credit cards generally offer stronger dispute rights than debit cards under federal law. See how to dispute a subscription charge.
  • Virtual card numbers, offered by some card issuers and third-party services, let you create a card number for a single merchant and lock or delete it later. Treat this as a backup, not a substitute for canceling: blocking a card does not cancel your agreement, and the merchant may still say you owe money.
  • Avoid using the card that pays your important bills, so a blocked or declined renewal does not create headaches elsewhere.

Common free trial traps

  • "Free" trials with a shipping charge. Some product trials, such as supplements or skincare, charge shipping and then enroll you in an expensive recurring plan. The FTC has repeatedly taken action against deceptive offers like these. Read every line before paying anything.
  • Introductory prices that look like trials. "$1 for the first month" is not a free trial, and the regular price after that may be much higher.
  • Annual conversions. A trial that converts to a yearly plan can mean a large one-time charge you will not notice until your statement arrives.
  • Pre-checked boxes and add-ons during checkout that enroll you in extra services.
  • Cancel-by-phone-only policies with limited hours. If you see this, plan to cancel well before the deadline.
  • Bundled trials. Signing up for one service can include a trial of another — such as a streaming add-on — that renews separately.

After you cancel

  1. Get confirmation. Save the cancellation email or screenshot the confirmation screen showing the end date.
  2. Check your next statement to make sure no charge appears.
  3. Delete stored payment info from the account if the service allows it.
  4. If you are charged anyway, contact the company first, then follow the steps in our dispute guide.
