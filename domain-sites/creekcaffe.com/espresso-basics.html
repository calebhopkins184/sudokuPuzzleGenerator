Espresso Basics: How to Pull a Great Shot at Home

Espresso is coffee brewed by forcing hot water through a compact bed of finely ground coffee at high pressure, typically about 9 bar. A reliable starting recipe is 18 grams of coffee in, about 36 grams of liquid espresso out (a 1:2 ratio), in roughly 25–30 seconds. You then adjust the grind until the shot tastes balanced.

By the Creek Caffe editorial team · Updated November 2, 2026

Starting espresso recipe (double shot)

Dose
18 g finely ground coffee (adjust to your basket size)
Yield
About 36 g of espresso in the cup
Ratio
1:2
Time
25–30 seconds from pump start
Water
About 90–96°C (machine setting)

The three numbers: dose, yield, time

Dialing in espresso means controlling three numbers. Dose is the dry coffee in the basket. Yield is the weight of liquid espresso in the cup — weigh it on a scale, as volume is misleading because of crema. Time is how long the shot takes. Keep dose and target yield fixed, and use grind size to change the time.

Common espresso styles by ratio
StyleRatio (in:out)Character
RistrettoAbout 1:1 to 1:1.5Syrupy, intense, sweeter, less bitter
NormaleAbout 1:2Balanced; the usual starting point
LungoAbout 1:3Lighter body, more extraction, can turn bitter

Pulling a shot step by step

  1. Warm up. Let the machine fully heat, often 15–30 minutes for many home machines, with the portafilter locked in.
  2. Dose. Grind fresh coffee into the dry portafilter basket and weigh it.
  3. Distribute. Break up clumps and level the grounds. A simple tool or stirring with a fine needle (often called WDT) helps prevent channeling.
  4. Tamp. Press down firmly and level. Consistency matters more than force.
  5. Flush. Run a short burst of water through the group head to stabilize temperature.
  6. Brew. Lock in, place the cup on a scale, start the shot and a timer. Stop at your target yield.
  7. Taste and adjust. Then clean the basket and group head.

Dialing in: fixing sour or bitter shots

Espresso troubleshooting
ShotTasteFix
Runs fast (under ~20 s)Sour, thin, saltyGrind finer
Runs slow (over ~35 s), dripsBitter, harsh, ashyGrind coarser
Normal time but sourUnder-extractedTry a longer ratio (more yield) or hotter water
Normal time but bitterOver-extractedTry a shorter ratio or cooler water; check roast freshness
Sprays, spurts, unevenSour and bitter togetherImprove distribution and tamping (channeling)

Shot time is a guideline, not a rule. If a 22-second shot tastes delicious, it’s a good shot. Light roasts often need finer grinds, hotter water and longer ratios; dark roasts usually like slightly cooler water and shorter ratios.

Milk drinks

Steam milk by placing the wand tip just below the surface to “stretch” it with air for a few seconds, then submerge it slightly to spin the milk into a glossy whirlpool. Stop at about 55–65°C (130–150°F) — the pitcher is too hot to hold comfortably for more than a moment. Hotter than that, milk loses sweetness and can scald.

  • Macchiato: espresso “marked” with a spoonful of foam.
  • Cortado: espresso with a roughly equal amount of steamed milk.
  • Flat white: double espresso with a thin layer of silky microfoam, smaller than a latte.
  • Cappuccino: espresso with steamed milk and a thicker foam layer.
  • Latte: espresso with more steamed milk and a thin foam layer.

Gear that matters

  • Grinder first: espresso needs a grinder that can make small, precise adjustments at fine settings. A great machine with a poor grinder gives poor espresso.
  • Machine types: manual lever machines, semi-automatic machines (you start and stop the shot), automatic and super-automatic machines (built-in grinder, one-touch), and capsule machines (convenient, less control).
  • Scale: a small scale that fits under the portafilter makes dialing in far easier.
  • Tamper: one that fits your basket diameter snugly.

Moka pots and AeroPress “espresso”

A stovetop moka pot makes strong, rich coffee, but it brews at much lower pressure than an espresso machine, so it isn’t true espresso and won’t produce the same crema. An AeroPress can make a concentrated, espresso-style coffee that works well in milk drinks. Both are excellent, affordable options; see camp coffee methods for using them outdoors.

Cleaning and maintenance

  • Wipe the group head and rinse the portafilter after each session.
  • Purge and wipe the steam wand immediately after each use.
  • Backflush with a blind basket if your machine supports it (check the manual).
  • Descale as your manufacturer recommends, based on water hardness, or use suitable filtered water to reduce scale.

Quick answers

What is the standard espresso ratio?

The usual starting point is 1:2: for example, 18 grams of ground coffee producing about 36 grams of espresso in roughly 25 to 30 seconds.

Why does my espresso taste sour?

Sour espresso is usually under-extracted, often because the shot ran too fast. Grind finer, increase the yield slightly or raise the water temperature.
